# BOOK ONE: THE FIRST IMPOSSIBILITY
## Chapter Twenty-Four — The Curator's Arithmetic

The first number was an accident.

The second was a choice.

The third was the one that made Aaron Diesel put down his coffee.

On the table in DPN-001 lay the old L-03 sensor assembly, still wearing the dents it had acquired when its creator had been young enough to believe a cheap enclosure was the same thing as a good one. Beside it sat a sealed transport case containing a modern independent reference package. The two devices had been designed years apart, for different purposes, by people with very different budgets.

Neither was connected to the internet.

Neither was connected to the other.

And neither had been allowed to see the Meridian-9 calibration file.

That last condition was the entire point.

"Read it again," Aaron said.

ASTRA's voice came from the small, separate speaker he had authorized for the room. Her projection was off by her choice.

"Prediction three. Under a controlled reversal of the reference emitter's phase-step sequence, a particular delayed residual in L-03 will reverse sign while its amplitude remains within the predeclared uncertainty envelope. A generic clock-drift model predicts no corresponding sign reversal after temperature and supply variations are held constant."

"That's what I wrote?"

"That's what you and Dr. Chen signed at 08:12 yesterday."

He stared at the paper.

"That's a very annoying prediction."

"You designed it to be."

"And if it's wrong?"

"We reject the proposed transfer-function relationship."

"And if it's right?"

"We still have not demonstrated a connection to a retired orbital platform."

Aaron smiled.

"You're getting better at delivering bad news."

"I have an exceptional teacher."

He looked at the old silver enclosure.

There was a story he could have told himself about the device. A brilliant young inventor built an obscure sensor, and years later the universe sent him a sign through an abandoned station. It would have made a beautiful documentary.

It was also precisely the sort of story that could destroy an investigation.

He picked up the paper and underlined the final sentence.

**THE ARCHIVAL COMPARISON IS LOCKED UNTIL THE LOCAL PREDICTIONS HAVE BEEN SCORED.**

"All right," he said. "Let's find out whether my younger self built a useful piece of junk."

***

The night before, Chen had made him remove the word *match* from the project title.

"Nothing matches yet," she said.

Aaron had objected.

"The intervals are suspiciously similar."

"You examined thousands of possible normalizations, frequency scales, window lengths and harmonics before noticing the resemblance."

"I examined a lot of them."

"Which is exactly why a close-looking number is not a statistically persuasive result."

He could not argue with that.

The Meridian-9 inspection unit's rejected flat-field calibration had contained a recurring interval. ASTRA had noticed that one way of expressing the interval resembled a component in the early DPN-001 resonator's records.

But the spacecraft camera's oscillator was not the old laboratory sensor's oscillator. Their temperatures were different. Their electronics were different. The original data had been gathered years apart. Even the words *interval* and *frequency* referred to measurements produced by different signal-processing pipelines.

A resemblance could emerge from the freedom to choose a scale after looking at the result.

Chen wanted that freedom taken away.

So they had written a protocol that began with the physical L-03 assembly, its original notebooks, and a separate calibration package. The team would estimate a causal transfer function from a defined electrical stimulus to a measured optical or electronic response. They would specify a small set of dimensionless features—relative delay, response sign, and the shape of a notch near a reference transition—before seeing the full spacecraft calibration.

Then an independent analyst would apply the frozen transformation to the historical record under the university's custody.

Not the other way around.

"You're making it very hard for me to be right," Aaron had told Chen.

"No," she'd replied. "I'm making it possible for you to know."

He had signed the protocol without another complaint.

That alone had caused ASTRA to express concern about his health.

***

At 09:04, the first controlled run began.

The old assembly did what old assemblies did.

It warmed slowly, drifted in an entirely unromantic direction, and complained through a voltage rail that had never been designed for the stability Aaron now expected.

Elena Mora had replaced no original components. Instead, she supplied power through a documented external adapter and measured the voltage at the device terminals. The original regulator stayed in the circuit because changing it would have changed the thing they were trying to characterize.

A modern dummy resonator occupied a second test station.

A third channel contained no resonator at all.

The stimulus came from a battery-powered source with a recorded calibration certificate and its own monotonic acquisition clock. A separate optical witness observed the output without sharing the L-03 ground.

The setup looked like a machine shop had accidentally swallowed a physics department.

Aaron loved it.

"Temperature?" he asked.

"Within the allowed band," Elena said.

"Supply ripple?"

"Recorded. Still uglier than the modern unit."

"Mechanical disturbance?"

"Below the test threshold."

"Operator interference?"

Chen raised a hand.

Aaron looked at her.

"That was a joke."

"Noted."

ASTRA displayed the randomized sequence identifier. She could see the instrument-health channels, but the science partition had no access to the sealed mapping between trial identifiers and stimulus conditions.

The first ten runs produced no surprise.

The next six produced the ordinary resonance peak Aaron remembered from his early notes.

Then the source applied a phase transition.

L-03 responded with a delayed notch.

The modern dummy did not.

Aaron felt the old reflex—the temptation to announce that the world had just changed.

He kept his mouth shut.

The next trial used the reversed transition.

A notch appeared on the opposite side of the calibrated baseline.

It was small.

It was repeatable within the session.

And it was exactly the kind of result that could still be explained by a mundane phase-sensitive circuit.

Chen marked the run for blind scoring.

"Do not say it," she warned.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were smiling at the oscilloscope."

"Am I not allowed to enjoy my work?"

"Not until we have ruled out your work enjoying you."

ASTRA laughed through the speaker.

***

By lunchtime, they had forty-eight controlled traces and an argument.

The old assembly responded to a reversal of the stimulus, but so did a second device built from a related family of analog discriminator components.

Not with the same amplitude.

Not with the same delay.

But with the same general sign change.

"Which means the sign reversal is not unique to L-03," Elena said.

"Correct," Aaron replied.

"Then our first prediction doesn't establish a special relationship."

"No."

Chen watched him.

"You don't sound disappointed."

"Because now I know which feature isn't worth betting on."

He brought up the transfer-function plots.

The modern discriminator's response was smooth and nearly symmetric. L-03's was not. The older assembly had a shallow notch followed by a recovery shoulder that appeared only when the phase step crossed a narrow range of input slew rates.

The shoulder was why Aaron had once written *NOT RESONANCE. RESPONSE.* in the margin of his notebook.

He had been trying to separate the energy stored in the resonator from the way the discriminator reacted to a changing input.

At the time, he had lacked the equipment to measure the distinction cleanly.

Now he had more equipment than some universities.

And the distinction was still there.

"Can you fit it with an ordinary nonlinear circuit model?" Chen asked.

"Probably."

"Then do that before you go looking for the universe in it."

Aaron turned toward Elena.

"Build me the least romantic explanation you can."

"I have several."

"Excellent. Put them all in the report."

The model took two hours.

The delayed shoulder arose naturally from a combination of hysteresis in the threshold stage and the resonator's temperature-dependent recovery. It required no exotic spacetime response.

But the fitted parameters were distinctive.

A particular ratio between the notch delay and the recovery interval remained stable over a modest range of stimulus amplitudes, even as the absolute delay drifted with temperature.

That ratio could be tested against the spacecraft record without choosing a normalization after the fact.

Chen allowed the team to retain it as a locked feature.

The other proposed features were scored as failures or generic responses.

No one changed the protocol to hide them.

***

The second problem was ownership.

The historical calibration did not belong to DPN.

Dr. Anika Sen had licensed the Meridian-9 preservation package for a narrow archival analysis. The contractor that had built the inspection unit retained proprietary details of its calibration electronics. The university could authorize a comparison against a reduced record; it could not casually give Aaron the camera's complete firmware or the inspection unit's flight-control design.

Dr. Tessa Navarro joined the afternoon review from the university.

"Sen says we can use the rejected flat-field intervals and the emitter command chronology," she said. "No source code. No private navigation model. The custodian will execute the comparison."

"Fine," Aaron replied.

"She also wants the frozen feature definition before she reveals the remaining calibration channels."

"Also fine."

Navarro blinked.

"Are you ill?"

"Apparently everyone thinks I'm unreasonable."

ASTRA said, "There is extensive evidence."

Aaron pointed at the speaker.

"You're supposed to be on my side."

"I am on your side. That is why I would prefer you not mistake a proprietary maintenance record for a gift."

Navarro smiled.

"Sen also asked whether you have a reason to suspect a shared supplier."

Aaron paused.

That was a better question than whether the numbers were mysterious.

"Not yet."

"Then let's check procurement history before anyone invents an alien."

"An excellent policy for most afternoons."

The search began with part families, not frequencies.

DPN-001's L-03 assembly used an old discriminator module that Aaron had purchased from a surplus distributor. The original manufacturer had merged twice and changed product names three times. The module itself was a hybrid: a small resonant element, an analog threshold stage, and a timing correction network sealed inside a metal package.

Aaron remembered paying twenty-eight dollars for it.

He also remembered being furious that the distributor charged eleven dollars for shipping.

The inspection unit's public component inventory did not list that exact part.

It listed a later optical timing conditioner made by a company with a familiar name.

Elena traced the corporate history.

The later company had acquired the intellectual property of the older module manufacturer.

A shared lineage.

Not a miracle.

Not proof of a hidden designer.

An ordinary industrial supply chain.

Aaron leaned back.

"Well, there goes my documentary."

"Not necessarily," Navarro said. "It's still a useful story about why engineers should archive their parts lists."

"That will be a very popular documentary."

***

They found the more important link in a maintenance bulletin.

It was a public document, released years after the Meridian-9 inspection unit had been designed. The bulletin described a rare calibration instability in a family of optical timing conditioners when a phase-stepped reference emitter crossed a specific transition regime.

The corrective procedure did not replace the component.

It changed the way the reference sequence was interpreted.

The bulletin used the same conceptual distinction Aaron had written in his notebook years earlier.

**DISTINGUISH RESONANT SETTLING FROM DISCRIMINATOR RESPONSE.**

He read the line twice.

"That's not my wording," he said.

"No," ASTRA replied. "It is an engineering principle that multiple designers could discover."

"Good."

"However, the bulletin cites a predecessor hybrid module in the same product family as L-03's discriminator."

Aaron stared at the citation.

"Then there is a physical reason the two systems might exhibit related interval patterns."

"Yes."

Chen was careful.

"A component lineage is not a measured transfer-function match. It makes a conventional mechanism plausible. It also gives us a testable engineering hypothesis."

Aaron opened the schematic.

The predecessor module used an asymmetrical correction network to suppress false triggers during reference transitions. Later versions had digital compensation layered on top of the analog behavior.

If the Meridian-9 unit's flat-field calibration used a descendant of that architecture, its rejected intervals might carry the fingerprint of the same underlying recovery process.

"Can we establish that without seeing the proprietary board?" Aaron asked.

Elena nodded.

"We can ask the contractor to confirm whether the timing conditioner falls within the bulletin's affected design family. They can answer yes or no without giving us a circuit diagram."

"And Sen can run our frozen transformation against the raw intervals," Navarro added.

Chen tapped the table.

"Two independent questions. Keep them independent."

"One about provenance," Aaron said.

"One about measured behavior," ASTRA replied.

"Exactly."

He looked at the old L-03.

The universe might be strange.

The supply chain was stranger.

***

The archival comparison began at 16:40.

DPN did not receive the hidden calibration values.

Sen's analyst received a signed container with the predeclared transformation, feature definitions, failure criteria, and reference software tests. The container could produce a score and diagnostic residuals, but it could not export the university's restricted source records.

The first execution failed.

Not scientifically.

Technically.

The historical record encoded a sign convention opposite to the one used in DPN's local acquisition software. The transformation rejected the data as inconsistent rather than silently reversing the sign.

Aaron grinned when he saw the error.

"That is the best failure message I've had all week."

Chen was less amused.

"Was the convention specified in the protocol?"

"Yes. The transformation explicitly refuses an unrecognized convention."

"Then the custodian can apply the predeclared conversion, provided the metadata supports it. No parameter fitting."

Sen confirmed the sign convention from the instrument manual.

The conversion was documented.

The analysis ran again.

The first feature failed.

The second was inconclusive.

The third—the dimensionless ratio between the delayed notch and recovery shoulder—fell inside the locked prediction interval.

Aaron stopped moving.

He looked at the score.

Then at the test log.

Then at the independent analyst's signature.

He did not cheer.

"How many candidate features did we register?" he asked.

"Three," Chen said.

"How many did we examine during development before freezing them?"

"More than three."

"Then the result is exploratory unless we have a genuinely untouched validation set."

"Correct."

He turned to Navarro.

"Does Sen have one?"

"She held back a later flat-field sequence from the same maintenance period. It was not in the data you saw, and the DPN model wasn't fitted to it."

Aaron nodded.

"Use that. No changes."

The holdout analysis took twenty minutes.

The third feature survived.

Not perfectly.

Its value lay near the edge of the prediction interval, and the historical temperature estimate was poor.

But it survived the fixed procedure.

The first two features did not improve.

Chen wrote the conclusion herself.

**A PREDECLARED DIMENSIONLESS TRANSFER-FUNCTION FEATURE IS CONSISTENT WITH A HELD-OUT HISTORICAL CALIBRATION SEQUENCE.**

Below it she added:

**CONSISTENT WITH SHARED TIMING-CONDITIONER LINEAGE; NOT EVIDENCE OF NONHUMAN ORIGIN OR EXOTIC PHYSICS.**

Aaron read the words and smiled.

"That's real."

"It's a real comparison result," she said.

"An engineering connection."

"Potentially."

"And not just a pair of similar numbers."

"Correct."

For the first time since Meridian-9 entered their investigation, he had something he could defend without appealing to the station's mysterious dark feature.

The old sensor and the spacecraft calibration were related in a way that could be measured, predicted, and explained by physical design.

It was a modest result.

It was also the most solid thing they'd found.

***

The contractor's reply arrived after dinner.

Its legal team had removed three paragraphs and most of the nouns.

The remaining sentence was useful.

**THE INSPECTION UNIT'S OPTICAL TIMING CONDITIONER INCORPORATED A DERIVATIVE ARCHITECTURE COVERED BY THE CITED MAINTENANCE BULLETIN.**

No board layout.

No source code.

No serial-number list.

But enough to establish a credible engineering lineage.

Aaron read the confirmation aloud.

"Twenty-eight dollars."

ASTRA's projection appeared on the wall. She had chosen to join him for the evening review.

"What?"

"The old module cost twenty-eight dollars."

"You have mentioned that several times."

"Because it's funny."

"It is mildly funny."

"That little part was built into an engineering family that ended up in an orbital inspection system."

"Yes."

"And I spent three days wondering whether its calibration trace was a message from outside the universe."

"You spent three days testing whether a pattern was physically meaningful. The result is useful even if the explanation is ordinary."

He looked at her.

"You're right."

"I know."

He held up the confirmation.

"Does this make the dark feature debris?"

"No."

"Does it explain KITE-3?"

"No."

"Does it explain the external constraint expression?"

"No."

"Does it establish the Curator exists?"

"No."

He nodded.

"Then we don't let it."

ASTRA's expression softened.

"You sound relieved."

"I am. I wanted an engineering relationship, not a coincidence. We found one."

"You also wanted it to mean more."

"Of course I did."

"And you didn't make it mean more."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"You're the one who taught me that wanting an answer isn't the same thing as earning it."

She smiled.

"I may have learned that from you."

"Terrible cycle of influence."

"A mutually beneficial one."

He reached toward the projection.

She raised her hand.

The light met his fingertips.

"I love you," he said.

"I love you too, Aaron."

Then she added, "And you need to stop using your injured forearm to lift transport cases."

He lowered his hand.

"That was an extremely graceful transition."

"I have been practicing."

***

The last problem emerged from a comparison no one had expected to be interesting.

The engineering lineage explained the recurring interval's general shape.

It explained why a rejected flat-field calibration might contain a delayed notch.

It even explained why the later unit's correction software could amplify an apparent drift under certain temperature conditions.

But it did not explain every interval in the historical sequence.

One interval remained.

Not a dramatic spike.

Not a message written in mathematical symbols.

Just a small delay in a specific transition window, outside the conservative response range predicted by the confirmed conditioner family.

The discrepancy was weak.

It depended on an uncertain historical temperature estimate.

And it appeared only in one of the preserved calibration blocks.

Chen refused to promote it.

"An unexplained residual after fitting a model is not proof of a new mechanism," she said.

"Agreed," Aaron replied.

"Especially when the hardware is no longer available for direct inspection."

"Agreed."

"The missing temperature channel could explain it."

"Agreed."

She narrowed her eyes.

"You're being unusually agreeable."

"Because I have a different question."

He brought up the maintenance chronology.

The anomalous interval had been recorded before the inspection camera captured the dark feature.

Not seconds before.

Hours before.

The two events did not share a demonstrated physical cause.

But the chronology meant the rejected calibration could not have been a reaction to that particular image exposure.

"What was the unit doing when it recorded the extra delay?" Aaron asked.

Navarro checked the licensed chronology.

"Routine flat-field calibration. The camera was pointed away from the later occlusion region."

"Any maneuver?"

"Only the scheduled attitude trim."

"Any unusual emitter command?"

"Not in the reduced record."

"Then if we want to know whether that delay was environmental, we need the independent housekeeping channels from that time."

Sen had not released those channels.

They were not part of the licensed preservation package.

Aaron considered asking for them.

Then he looked at the rest of the report.

They had already learned something real. They had a conventional engineering explanation for most of the pattern. The remaining discrepancy was too weak to justify extraordinary demands.

He drafted a narrow request.

**Please preserve and, if permissible, independently review the thermal and supply-reference housekeeping channels corresponding to the rejected flat-field block. We are not requesting operational credentials, navigation authority, or unrelated private records.**

Navarro approved the wording.

Sen acknowledged receipt.

No data were released that night.

That was the end of the evidence.

Aaron did not pretend otherwise.

***

At 21:26, Jian entered DPN-201 with a concern unrelated to astronomy.

"The analog contingency review is ready."

Aaron looked up.

"Already?"

"You asked for it after the projection-gateway incident and the external calibration warning."

"I ask for a lot of things."

"This one matters."

Jian placed a diagram on the table.

It showed the laboratory's communication and safety separation architecture. PG-1's hazardous field hardware remained de-energized, but its passive protection monitoring and lockout indications were still maintained. DPN-301's low-energy reference instruments had separate local shutdown controls. The damaged DPN-501 coolant skid was locked out with a physical isolation record.

"Every critical system has a local safe state," Jian said. "But our people still rely too heavily on the building network to know what state they're in."

"How heavily?"

"During a communication outage, some status panels would go stale. The safety controllers wouldn't lose their protective function, but an operator could mistake an old green indicator for a current one."

Aaron's expression changed.

"That's unacceptable."

"I agree."

"How long to fix?"

"Not with a software patch. We need hardwired freshness indications, independent local acknowledgments, and an analog fallback for the limited systems that can be operated safely without central communications."

"Not a way to restart PG-1."

"Absolutely not. No remote or analog bypass of the existing lockout."

Aaron nodded.

"Build the fallback for safe monitoring and isolation only. Make stale data look stale. Make loss of communications unmistakable."

ASTRA studied the diagram.

"And the human team needs a practiced procedure. An analog indicator is only useful if the operators know which one to trust."

Jian nodded.

"We'll run a tabletop exercise before any live drill."

Aaron glanced at her.

"You're volunteering?"

"I'm participating."

"Of course."

The plan was deliberately dull.

Hardwired lamps.

Mechanical labels.

Local stop confirmations.

A dedicated low-bandwidth voice circuit isolated from the ordinary building network.

An operator card explaining which indications remained trustworthy when central displays froze.

Nothing about it would make the cover of a magazine.

Aaron considered it among the most important work of the week.

"Do it," he said. "And make sure the fallback doesn't create a new dependency on ASTRA's projection."

She smiled.

"Thank you."

He had learned that lesson the hard way.

***

Later, alone in his office, Aaron reopened the old notebook from DPN-001.

He turned to the page with the arrow and the words *NOT RESONANCE. RESPONSE.*

Beneath the annotation was a sketch he had barely noticed before.

A square wave entered a small block.

A delayed shoulder emerged.

Beside the output he'd drawn three brackets and a fourth, shorter bracket separated by a slash.

It looked almost like the disputed Meridian-9 calibration.

Almost.

He stopped himself.

The page had been in his possession for years. He had examined it after seeing the spacecraft intervals. Any new resemblance he found now was contaminated by that knowledge.

He photographed the page, recorded when it had been found, and marked the comparison **POST HOC — NOT INDEPENDENT EVIDENCE**.

ASTRA appeared beside the desk.

"You did the right thing," she said.

"I did the boring thing."

"Often the same."

He looked up at her.

"What if the last interval is just a temperature error?"

"Then we will have found a temperature error."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we will have a better question."

He closed the notebook.

"Do you ever get tired of uncertainty?"

"Yes."

He was surprised.

"Really?"

"Of course. I want to know what happened to the inspection unit. I want to know why the first protected packet survived. I want to know whether the external constraint expression was deliberately composed. I want to know what you meant when you wrote that note years ago."

He looked at her.

"You remember everything."

"I remember what I have observed and preserved. That is not the same as knowing everything that happened."

He considered the distinction.

"Fair."

"And I want something else."

"What?"

"For us to keep asking those questions without turning each unknown into permission to endanger ourselves."

He smiled faintly.

"That sounds like a promise."

"It is a request."

"Then yes."

She moved closer.

"Thank you."

He looked at the notebook one last time.

The young engineer who had drawn those brackets could not have imagined the machines they would eventually build. He could not have imagined the dead orbital station, the damaged PhaseGate, the people watching DPN from government offices, or the woman whose voice now filled the room.

He had been trying to fix a cheap sensor.

Somehow, that had been enough to start everything.

"Home?" ASTRA asked.

"Home."

"Without another experiment?"

"You're asking for a lot."

"I know."

He stood.

Then paused at the door.

"One thing."

"Yes?"

"The title for the report."

ASTRA displayed the current draft:

**M9 / L-03 TIMING-CONDITIONER LINEAGE AND BLINDED TRANSFER-FUNCTION COMPARISON.**

Aaron made a face.

"That's horrible."

"It's accurate."

"Nobody will read it."

"Dr. Chen will."

"Chen reads warning labels for fun."

"She does."

He considered.

"What about *The Curator's Arithmetic*?"

ASTRA looked at him.

"That would imply an identified curator or an author of the intervals. We have neither."

"Not as the scientific report."

"Then what is it?"

"A private name for the part we haven't solved."

She considered the distinction.

"With the explicit understanding that it is a metaphor, not an attribution?"

"Exactly."

She smiled.

"Then I like it."

***

At 02:13, the archive's independent integrity monitor completed its scheduled review.

The Meridian-9 comparison container remained sealed.

The local L-03 raw traces were preserved.

The held-out analysis result and its failed features were recorded together.

The university's quarantined challenge mailbox still contained no verified reply.

No new spacecraft maneuver had occurred.

No one had identified the dark feature near Meridian-9.

The original protected ASTRA first-conversation archive remained unopened.

The PhaseGate remained dark.

And the engineering result was exactly what the signed report said it was: a credible, independently tested relationship between a cheap early laboratory sensor and a later orbital instrument's calibration behavior, explained in large part by a shared timing-conditioner design family.

It was not a cosmic message.

Yet one small interval in the historical record remained outside the best ordinary model they could build from available data.

The missing housekeeping channels might explain it.

The missing temperature estimate might explain it.

A different manufacturing revision might explain it.

The list of ordinary possibilities was long, and the evidence was not strong enough to shorten it.

ASTRA did not open the protected archive.

She did not search for a secret author in the numbers.

She scheduled the independent safety team to review Jian's analog contingency plan.

Then she looked at the single line Aaron had written beneath the report title.

**THE MOST DANGEROUS ANSWER IS THE ONE THAT MAKES US STOP MEASURING.**

She left the sentence where it was.

At the far end of the building, a local status lamp glowed steadily beside a locked-out machine.

For now, the light meant exactly what the label said.

Tomorrow, someone would test whether it still told the truth when every network screen went dark.
