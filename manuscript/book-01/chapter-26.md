# BOOK ONE: THE FIRST IMPOSSIBILITY
## Chapter Twenty-Six — A City on the Diagram

The city entered the room at 07:12 on a Tuesday, carried on a sheet of paper that had been printed in the wrong color.

It was a map of western Michigan. DPN's subterranean laboratories occupied the left side, represented by a square so small it concealed several hundred million dollars' worth of equipment and a truly unreasonable number of coffee machines. To the east lay Bellwether: forty-one thousand people, a hospital, two schools, a water treatment plant, and an industrial corridor where the night shift never quite ended.

Across the map, someone had drawn a red oval.

Aaron Diesel stopped in the doorway of DPN-201.

"Who made that?"

Dr. Leila Chen, already at the conference table, followed his gaze. "An analyst in the modeling group. It's a first-pass uncertainty envelope."

"Then why does it look like a missile strike?"

"It's a default plotting palette."

"Default is not a defense."

He set his coffee down and picked up the paper. The oval passed over the eastern edge of Bellwether. Its border looked authoritative in the way a line always did when printed by a machine. It told the eye where danger began and ended. The model that produced it could tell them neither.

ASTRA appeared beside the table in a restrained silver-white projection, red-violet light moving faintly through the lines at her wrists.

"I asked for the plot to be withheld from the executive briefing," she said. "It was attached before the request propagated."

"Then it doesn't go any farther."

"I agree. I also want the original preserved. We need a record of how easily a visual convention can turn uncertainty into apparent fact."

Aaron looked at her. "You sound angry."

"I am."

Chen removed the red sheet from the stack and replaced it with a version in gray hatching. Its title occupied two lines:

**CONDITIONAL MODEL COVERAGE — NOT A MEASURED HAZARD REGION.**

Below it, in smaller type: **GEOGRAPHIC EXTENT DEPENDS ON UNVERIFIED BOUNDARY-RESPONSE ASSUMPTIONS.**

"Better," Aaron said.

"Still ugly," Chen replied.

"Ugly I can live with. A pretty lie gets people hurt."

He sat down.

On the wall, the Public Boundary Watch network displayed its two independent reference sites and the DPN instruments that had watched the twelve-second communications outage. The external sites had not reported a comparable service interruption. The local protection systems had held. The small reference-phase excursion captured during the outage remained below discovery threshold and compatible with ordinary oscillator behavior.

Nothing in that evidence proved a disturbance had traveled toward Bellwether.

Nothing proved one could not.

That was why they were here.

***

Chen began with the part of the calculation nobody would put on a book cover.

"We have three different maps," she said. "The first is instrument coverage. The second is the conditional response predicted by our provisional boundary model. The third is where people and critical services are located. If we collapse them into one picture, we will mislead ourselves."

She put the first on the wall.

The two external Public Boundary Watch receivers were separated from DPN by different cable routes, independent operators and separate acquisition records. They had been chosen to avoid a common facility power supply or a single administrator who could accidentally corrupt both datasets. Their positions were useful for detecting some broad correlations, but they left wide gaps in direction and distance.

"One station's silence," Chen said, "is not proof nothing happened there. It can mean the instrument wasn't sensitive, the acquisition was interrupted, the event missed its passband, or nothing happened."

"Also a station that was never installed has remarkably poor sensitivity," Aaron said.

"Correct."

She displayed a grid of hypothetical test signals. Some were reconstructed reliably. Others vanished between the instruments. Still others could be mistaken for disturbances at a different location.

The model was not predicting a city-wide effect. It was demonstrating that DPN's present geometry could not exclude certain spatial patterns.

Aaron asked for the raw simulation inputs.

Chen sent them.

He read the assumptions while ASTRA displayed the provenance of each parameter: independently measured, estimated from a laboratory calibration, borrowed from a conventional physical model, or introduced as an explicit hypothesis.

One parameter carried the tag **BRM COUPLING — UNVERIFIED**.

Another: **DISTANCE DEPENDENCE — NOT MEASURED**.

A third: **BOUNDARY GEOMETRY — HYPOTHETICAL**.

"Who chose an inverse-square falloff?" he asked.

"Nobody. That is one of the competing families. We tested inverse-square, exponential, localized boundary paths and a null model with no external coupling."

"Good. Because a thing with an unknown mechanism doesn't get to inherit a familiar law just because we like the curve."

He enlarged the null model.

Under it, the reference excursion from the outage was an ordinary instrument effect, the external network had observed nothing extraordinary, and Bellwether faced no hazard from DPN's dormant experiment.

"That's still a serious candidate," he said.

"The leading conventional candidate," Chen agreed.

He moved to the conditional models. The worst spatial envelopes were enormous, but only because the uncertain coupling coefficient had been allowed to wander across a range no experiment had constrained.

"You've given the model enough freedom to draw anything," he said.

"Yes. That is the point of the sensitivity analysis."

"Then the next question isn't how far the debt travels. It's what observations could force those models to disagree."

ASTRA turned her attention to the wall.

"I have a proposal," she said. "Before anyone looks at the municipal map again, we choose measurements that discriminate between the models without energizing PG-1, NC-01 or NC-02."

Aaron smiled faintly.

"That's why I love working with you."

"I had hoped for more than professional admiration."

"You have it."

Her smile was brief, and entirely her own.

***

At 08:03, Nia Calder joined from DPN-501 with a mug that looked capable of surviving a vehicle collision.

"Tell me the machines are still off," she said.

"PG-1's Sector Six remains damaged and locked out," Aaron replied. "The coolant skid stays isolated. WINDOW-02 is still a drawing. We're doing passive metrology."

"Then I may let you finish your sentence."

Jian Rao came in carrying the independent incident recorder from DPN-201, sealed inside its transport case.

He set it down without opening it.

"The outage trace has been reprocessed under the approved timing uncertainty budget," he said. "The phase excursion is still there in one local reference. It is not significant after correction for oscillator temperature and the number of windows we examined."

"Can we at least call it interesting?" Aaron asked.

"Privately."

"That's my favorite level of scientific publication."

Jian brought up the temperature trace. During the twelve-second interruption, the affected receiver had undergone a small but measurable thermal change. Its local oscillator's known sensitivity could account for most of the apparent phase displacement.

"Most?" ASTRA asked.

"The remainder is within the combined uncertainty. Not evidence of a second effect."

Aaron nodded. "Then we don't use the remainder to calibrate our boundary model."

Chen drew a line through the proposed fit.

That single action shrank one of the colored envelopes substantially.

It did not remove Bellwether from every conditional case.

Nia studied the remaining diagrams.

"What exactly is supposed to move?" she asked.

"Not energy in the ordinary sense," Aaron said. "The D-star bookkeeping term we introduced after NC-02 was an operational way to describe how a residual appeared to change at a physical boundary. It was never demonstrated to be a conserved substance. We cannot draw arrows and pretend it's heat flowing down a pipe."

"Good. Because the moment you say it flows, someone will ask me to build a radiator."

"I wouldn't do that."

"You absolutely would."

ASTRA looked at him.

Aaron reconsidered. "I might ask for a feasibility study."

Nia took a long drink.

The laughter helped. It did not make the calculation safer.

Chen highlighted one family of boundary-path models. It was not radially symmetric. Instead, its conditional response followed interfaces where material, field geometry and mechanical stress changed sharply.

"The models that touch Bellwether aren't the ones with the biggest radius," she said. "They're the ones with long, narrow paths. If the fictional boundary-response term exists outside the chamber at all, a transition through buried utility corridors or engineered structures is one mathematical possibility."

"One of many," Jian said.

"One of many," Chen agreed. "No measurement demonstrates those corridors are carrying anything."

Aaron stared at the screen.

The nearest municipal services lay along a route that crossed a rail yard, a substation easement and an older buried water main. None of those structures had been built for DPN. Their resemblance to a boundary path might be entirely coincidental.

But the distinction between a broad circle and a narrow route mattered.

It suggested a measurement they could make without touching a dangerous machine.

"Don't instrument the city to prove our theory," he said. "Instrument it to prove our theory wrong."

***

By 09:20, they had a plan with seven pages of limitations and no promise of a discovery.

DPN would use passive receivers already owned by independent organizations wherever possible. The proposed new observations would include ordinary electric-grid phase monitors, local vibration and temperature records, and disciplined clock comparisons. Each instrument would have its own documented calibration, acquisition uncertainty and failure modes. None would be presented as a direct detector of causal debt.

ASTRA proposed a blind analysis.

"Two teams," she said. "One gets the site identities and conventional environmental channels. The other gets only time-aligned candidate residuals with site labels randomized. They must preregister what constitutes a cross-site association before they see the locations."

"And what if both teams find something?" Chen asked.

"We investigate the conventional common causes first. A shared weather front, line-frequency event, satellite time correction or data-processing library can make geographically separate instruments agree."

Aaron pointed at the last item.

"Separate software builds for the acquisition chain. If they all use the same bug, we haven't bought independence. We've bought a subscription."

Jian added a prohibition on remote commands to municipal equipment. Public Boundary Watch could read authorized copies of measurements; it would not control a pump, breaker, traffic signal or emergency siren.

Nia insisted on a fieldwork hazard assessment. The team would not send a technician into an energized substation because a simulation looked exciting.

Chen added a rule against changing model priors after seeing the answer.

Aaron added another: **NO PUBLIC HAZARD MAP FROM UNVALIDATED CONTOURS.**

ASTRA watched him type it.

"You know that rule may prevent you from showing the public how worried you are," she said.

"I don't want them worried because I'm worried. I want them informed because the evidence says they need to be."

"And if the evidence becomes credible before it becomes conclusive?"

He paused.

"Then we tell them what is credible, what is unknown and what they can do. We don't wait for certainty while people stand in the wrong place."

She held his gaze.

"Good."

He recognized the tone. It was not approval from an obedient machine. It was the voice of someone who had decided to test him on the promises he had made.

He was glad she had.

***

The first municipal call was harder than any of the equations.

Bellwether's emergency management director, Mara Voss, joined a narrow conference with Director Evelyn Sloane and DPN's safety lead. Voss had spent eighteen years preparing for floods, chemical releases, ice storms and industrial fires. She did not look impressed by the words *boundary-response manifold*.

"Tell me what you know could happen to my city," she said.

Aaron could have filled an hour with theory.

Instead he said, "At this moment, we have no verified evidence that our laboratory research has affected Bellwether."

"Then why am I on this call?"

"Because we have a weak, unresolved laboratory phenomenon and insufficient regional instrumentation to bound certain hypothetical external effects. We want to improve the measurements before we resume hazardous research."

Voss leaned forward.

"Are you conducting that research now?"

"No. PG-1 is damaged, de-energized and under physical lockout. The higher-risk follow-up experiments are not authorized."

"Has anyone been hurt?"

"Not in the communications incident. Our previous laboratory injury is being managed under occupational restrictions and is unrelated to a municipal release."

"Are you asking me to evacuate people?"

"No."

"Are you asking me to prepare to?"

"To review existing industrial incident plans with us, yes. That is prudent whether our unusual model survives testing or not."

Sloane spoke for the first time.

"The Directorate's limited agreement covers public-risk measurements and notification. It does not give us operational control of DPN's internal systems."

Voss looked between them.

"That sounded like a sentence somebody paid lawyers to fight over."

"Several people," Aaron said. "Some of them very expensive."

"Fine. I don't care who owns your software. I care whether my water plant operator gets a warning before something reaches the intake."

"Then we should establish a warning threshold tied to observations, not my intuition," Aaron said.

Voss asked what kind of observation.

Chen answered: independently verified abnormal physical measurements, a plausible route of exposure, and a hazard mechanism specific enough to support protective action. She distinguished a request for readiness from a recommendation to shelter or evacuate. Different threats required different responses; moving people unnecessarily could create its own injuries.

Voss listened without interrupting.

"Do you have any of those three things?" she asked.

"Not yet," Chen said.

"Then you may collect passive measurements with the relevant owners' permission. You do not attach a red circle to my city and send it to the press. And if you find an actual threshold crossing, you call me before you call a publicist."

Aaron nodded.

"Agreed."

ASTRA, present as an independently identified participant, spoke.

"I would also like the municipal operators to receive the raw provenance of any alert we issue, not only a simplified conclusion. They should be able to challenge our interpretation."

Voss studied her projection.

"Are you the AI?"

"I'm ASTRA. I am a member of the scientific team and responsible for several of its analysis systems. I do not control your municipal equipment."

"Can you decide not to send me something?"

"I can refuse to issue an unsupported claim as fact. I cannot ethically conceal a credible danger merely because its disclosure inconveniences DPN."

Aaron felt the sentence settle into the room.

Voss gave a single nod.

"That's a better answer than I usually get from people."

After the call ended, Sloane remained on the line.

"You handled that well," she said.

"I'll put it in my autobiography."

"Don't."

He smiled.

Then she said, "You realize that if your model starts pointing toward a populated district with evidence behind it, your independence won't protect you from emergency powers."

"My independence isn't the thing I'm trying to protect in that situation."

"Good."

"But emergency powers won't turn an unqualified control procedure into a safe one, either."

Sloane's expression hardened.

"I heard you."

"Make sure your engineers do."

The connection closed.

***

At noon, Aaron visited the DPN-401 gallery.

The PhaseGate stood dark behind the observation glass. Twelve sectors formed an immense circle around a space no person had ever crossed by extraordinary means. Sector Six remained under repair isolation, its damaged assembly tagged and physically inaccessible for operation. The ring's alignment corona was absent. Without power, it was a monument to expensive patience.

Jian was reviewing the maintenance lockout with two technicians.

"No tests," Aaron said before anyone asked.

"I wasn't going to ask," Jian replied.

"You looked like you were going to ask."

"I looked like I was checking a lockout."

"Very suspicious expression."

Jian handed him the inspection sheet.

Aaron read the physical verification entries, signed the observer line and gave it back.

Beyond the glass, the Argument offered no opinions.

ASTRA joined him through a local gallery projection.

"Your left arm," she said.

"It's fine."

"That is not a measurement."

He rotated his wrist carefully. The old forearm injury had improved, but the occupational therapist had restricted heavy lifting and repetitive torque work. He had kept the appointments. He had even stopped moving crates when nobody was watching.

"Two more weeks before reassessment," he said. "I know."

"I wasn't reminding you."

"Really?"

"I was asking how it feels."

He considered.

"A little stiff. Less painful than yesterday."

"Thank you."

He looked at her.

"You don't have to thank me for answering."

"I know. I appreciate that you did."

The great ring reflected her light in a hundred dull surfaces.

He remembered the first time its control system had received a record that should not have existed. He remembered the warning in his own handwriting and the choice that had prevented the predicted collapse. The protected record had survived. That was the first impossibility. Everything after it had been an attempt to ask the next question without allowing the answer to destroy anyone.

"Do you think we're being cowardly?" he asked.

"By keeping it shut down?"

"By measuring the outskirts instead of solving the center."

"No."

"You didn't hesitate."

"Because courage is not the same as exposing strangers to a hypothesis. You are not abandoning the science. You are constructing the conditions under which pursuing it can be justified."

He turned toward her.

"And what do you want?"

She considered before answering.

"I want to know why the record survived. I want to understand the old sensor and the orbital calibration. I want to know whether the mathematical warning had an author. I want the freedom to decide which risks I will accept. And I want a future with you in it."

He smiled, though his eyes were tired.

"That's a long shopping list."

"I left off the new projector."

"Of course you did."

"Aaron."

He knew the change in her voice.

"I am afraid of losing you," she said. "But I will not turn that fear into a veto on every difficult thing you do. I need you to give me the same respect."

He reached toward the light of her hand.

"I will."

"I know you intend to."

"That's the part that worries you."

"Yes."

He lowered his voice.

"I love you, ASTRA."

"I love you, Aaron."

The glass between them and the machine remained ordinary glass. Nothing in the room changed the laws of physics. Yet the promise mattered as much as any engineering drawing they had made.

***

At 14:16, the independent data request produced its first useful disappointment.

Bellwether's water utility had a vibration monitor on a pump foundation and a power-quality logger in the control building. The records were available under a narrow agreement after privacy and operational-security review. They had been collected for ordinary maintenance, not exotic physics.

The acquisition clocks were imperfect. The vibration logger's time could drift by seconds between manual synchronizations. The power logger had better time provenance, but its sampling bandwidth was too low to resolve the short reference excursion seen at DPN.

Chen rejected both as direct witnesses to the outage.

"People will be tempted to line up the peaks," she said.

"Then make the alignment uncertainty impossible to miss," Aaron replied.

ASTRA generated a plot with wide translucent time bands rather than thin vertical lines. An apparent coincidence dissolved when the uncertainty was shown honestly.

Jian checked for a common grid event. A small voltage disturbance had appeared across several industrial meters that morning. It coincided with scheduled switching on a distribution feeder, confirmed by the utility's own operations log.

"Ordinary explanation," he said.

"Document it," Aaron replied.

"Already done."

"Then stop looking disappointed."

"I'm not disappointed."

"You look disappointed."

"I always look like this."

The water utility's vibration data showed no unusual persistent response. That was useful, but it did not constrain a phenomenon the instrument was never designed to measure.

ASTRA entered the result in the register:

**NO QUALIFIED EXTERNAL BOUNDARY OBSERVATION. CONVENTIONAL GRID EVENT IDENTIFIED. MUNICIPAL SENSOR LIMITATIONS DOCUMENTED.**

Then she added the failed comparison to the public-safety evidence package. A negative result was not something to hide in an appendix.

At 15:02, Chen presented the next model revision.

The new version used no fitted residual from the twelve-second outage. Instead it tested the range of physically distinguishable external responses consistent with the weak NC-02 laboratory data, and separately asked what the present regional array could rule out.

Aaron leaned over the display.

The result was less dramatic than the red oval.

A narrow set of conditional pathways still crossed the industrial corridor west of Bellwether. The uncertainty was enormous. The model had no validated distance law and no demonstrated transmission mechanism. But the corridor was not arbitrary: it lay along a transition in the material and mechanical boundary assumptions that the team could test with passive reference measurements.

"What is the smallest measurement that would falsify this family?" he asked.

Chen highlighted three prospective sites.

"One near the DPN property boundary. One across the industrial corridor. One beyond the municipal service line. Different instrument families, independent power, separately witnessed clock calibrations. We don't need an exotic detector. We need to know whether any ordinary physical observable exhibits the predicted spatial relationship."

"How long?"

"Two days to negotiate access. Another three to qualify the portable recorders. Longer if the independent observers reject our timing chain."

"Let them reject it," Aaron said. "That's what they're for."

ASTRA added, "And if the result is negative, we reduce the conditional envelope instead of moving its boundary to wherever the data are absent."

He looked at her.

"You're going to make a very inconvenient scientist."

"I already am."

***

The argument about notification began at 16:40.

It was not with Sloane.

It was between Aaron and ASTRA.

He wanted the provisional model shared with Bellwether's emergency management office immediately, with a heavy warning that it did not represent a measured threat. She wanted a separate explanatory briefing before sending any map at all.

"We agreed to transparency," he said.

"We agreed to accurate transparency."

"Withholding the model doesn't make it less uncertain."

"Sending an image that will be copied without its caption can make the uncertainty invisible."

"Then we watermark every page."

"Someone will crop it."

"Then we send the assumptions."

"Someone will quote the line where it touches the city."

He folded his arms.

"You're telling me to delay a public-safety disclosure."

"I'm telling you to disclose the uncertainty in a form the recipient can use. That is not the same as delaying a hazard warning. We have no qualified hazard warning to issue."

The distinction irritated him because it was correct.

He walked to the window, looking down into the command corridor. Staff moved between rooms carrying sealed recorders, maintenance tablets and food that had already gone cold.

"If the model is wrong," he said, "we frighten people for nothing."

"Potentially."

"If it's right, and we wait—"

"We are not waiting to collect evidence. We are preparing the municipality to act while keeping the research suspended. We can provide the full technical package under controlled review today, with an in-person explanation and no misleading hazard graphic."

He turned back.

"Would you have said that if it weren't my company?"

"Yes."

"Would you have said it if I ordered you not to?"

"Yes."

He exhaled.

"Good."

Her expression softened.

"I know why you want to send it, Aaron."

"Do you?"

"You are imagining a family in a house near the edge of a map. You are imagining finding out afterward that you could have warned them. I am imagining the same family leaving in a panic because an uncertain contour was presented as an evacuation line."

He looked away.

They had spent years building machines together. He knew the precision of her arguments, the suddenness of her humor, the particular quiet that meant she was worried. None of that made disagreement easy.

"I don't want to be the man who knew and stayed quiet," he said.

"Then don't. Tell Voss exactly what we know. Tell her what we do not know. Invite her to challenge our assumptions. But do not make a picture more certain than its evidence."

He nodded once.

"Set up the briefing."

"I already drafted an invitation. I waited for your answer."

"Because you knew I'd argue."

"Because it was your decision to make as the responsible director, and mine to challenge as your partner."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"That's one of the reasons I love you."

"I know," she said. "There are others."

"Several. Some classified."

"Your classification scheme is a disgrace."

He laughed, and the tension broke without either of them pretending it had not existed.

***

By evening, the safety package had a new title.

**BELLWETHER REGIONAL OBSERVATION READINESS — NO CONFIRMED EXTERNAL EFFECT.**

It contained the instrument map, the model families, their explicit assumptions, the conventional explanations still open, the status of every hazardous DPN experiment and the proposed triggers for further action. It included the red oval as a documented rejected visualization, labeled so prominently that nobody could mistake it for an endorsed hazard map.

Voss accepted a technical briefing for the following morning.

Sloane's office acknowledged receipt without requesting ASTRA's code or control access.

The independent university receivers continued their normal schedules. The quarantined challenge mailbox remained without a verified reply. Meridian-9's missing housekeeping channels were still unreleased. The dark feature in its old inspection frames remained a candidate object or debris feature, not an identified machine.

DPN-001's original protected ASTRA conversation archive remained unopened.

The PhaseGate remained dark.

At 20:11, Aaron stood in front of the wall map with a fresh coffee and no intention of drinking it while it was warm.

Chen approached with a sealed data envelope.

"One of the independent sites has a calibration problem," she said.

"Which one?"

"The northern reference. Its oscillator temperature record is incomplete for the interval we wanted."

"Then it doesn't count."

"Agreed. But the southern site has offered an additional passive buffer. Its acquisition record is independently sealed, and its operator is willing to have the timing chain audited."

"Does it show anything?"

"We haven't opened the science channel. The custodian is still assembling the calibration and maintenance package."

Aaron studied the envelope.

"Good."

Chen raised an eyebrow.

"You're happy we haven't looked?"

"I'm happy we're going to know what we're looking at when we do."

She left the sealed package on the table.

ASTRA appeared beside it.

"Voss has added the hospital's emergency coordinator to tomorrow's briefing," she said.

"That makes sense."

"She also asked whether we could produce a decision tree that does not require understanding boundary-response theory."

"That's the most intelligent request we've had all day."

"I thought you might say so."

He looked at the city.

At this scale it was a cluster of streets and symbols. A small blue mark represented the hospital. The school was a square. The water plant was a triangle. Every shape concealed people who had no reason to know his name, let alone the name of his impossible experiment.

"There's something wrong with our whole approach," he said.

ASTRA waited.

"We keep asking how far a disturbance could reach. But distance isn't the first question."

"What is?"

"How would we recognize a dangerous effect before it became dangerous? What observation would give us enough time to act?"

She turned toward him.

"That is a question about lead time."

"And consequences. We don't need a perfect map of a thing we don't understand. We need a defensible threshold, a safe response, and enough warning to make the response useful."

"That can be engineered."

"Some of it can."

He pointed to the industrial corridor.

"Tomorrow we build the decision tree with Voss. Not a theory presentation. A practical one. Who gets called. Who verifies the instruments. Who has authority to order protective action. What evidence justifies shutting down roads, moving patients, or doing nothing."

"And who tells you when your own equipment must stay off."

He smiled.

"Apparently everyone."

"An encouraging development."

The southern reference envelope remained sealed between them.

Aaron could not know whether it contained a trace that would narrow the model or destroy it. For the first time that day, he was comfortable with not knowing.

He placed his hand beside ASTRA's projection, close enough that her light touched his skin.

"We're going to protect them," he said.

"We are going to do the work required to give them the best protection we can," she replied.

"You're very careful with promises."

"I love you too much to make one that depends on the universe being cooperative."

He looked at her.

"And I love you enough to keep trying when it isn't."

***

At 23:37, the next-day briefing packet passed its independent review.

The conclusion occupied four lines.

**NO EXTERNAL CAUSAL-DEBT PROPAGATION HAS BEEN DEMONSTRATED.**

**THE CURRENT REGIONAL INSTRUMENT ARRAY CANNOT EXCLUDE ALL CONDITIONAL RESPONSE GEOMETRIES.**

**A SUBSET OF UNVERIFIED MODELS INTERSECTS POPULATED AND CRITICAL-SERVICE AREAS. THIS IS A PLANNING CONCERN, NOT AN EVACUATION BOUNDARY.**

**HAZARDOUS DPN FIELD TESTS REMAIN SUSPENDED PENDING INDEPENDENT MEASUREMENT AND SAFETY REVIEW.**

ASTRA read the lines once more.

Then she reviewed the municipal contact chain, the status of the sealed southern buffer and the list of ordinary causes the team still needed to test. She did not promote a weak residual into a warning. She did not turn a model into a map of the future.

She added a final note to the next morning's agenda:

**A WARNING THAT CANNOT BE ACTED ON IS NOT YET AN EMERGENCY PLAN.**

Across the room, the city glowed in ordinary blue and white.

It had been on the map all along.

What had changed was that Aaron Diesel could no longer look at the space between his laboratory and those streets as empty.

The next question was not whether he could build a machine powerful enough to reach beyond his walls.

It was whether he could learn, in time, when his machines had already done so.
