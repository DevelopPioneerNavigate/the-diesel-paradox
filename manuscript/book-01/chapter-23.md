# BOOK ONE: THE FIRST IMPOSSIBILITY
## Chapter Twenty-Three — The Dead Station

The photograph arrived with a warning that was more frightening than anything in the photograph.

**DO NOT ASSUME THE IMAGE REPRESENTS A PHYSICAL OBJECT.**

Aaron Diesel read it twice.

Then he opened the file.

A retired survey platform hung against a field of stars, its long solar wings folded into the peculiar shape of a wounded insect. Meridian-9 had been built to listen to the sky. Its receivers had been shut down years ago, its station-keeping fuel rationed to a final disposal plan, and its last published position was old enough to be useful only as a warning.

Near one service truss was a dark patch.

Not a sphere.

Not a doorway.

Not a perfect black rectangle from the cover of a cheap science-fiction novel.

Just an irregular absence where the inspection camera expected to see the platform's sunlit insulation.

Aaron leaned closer.

"That's it?"

Dr. Tessa Navarro, appearing on the other side of the conference display, looked offended.

"That's the archival thumbnail. The actual inspection sequence is still under release review."

"How long?"

"Possibly days."

"Can we buy the days?"

"No."

"Rent them?"

"Also no."

ASTRA stood beside him in a projection she had chosen to open. The glass behind her held the photograph at life size.

"That patch may be a shadow cast by the service truss," she said.

"I know."

"Or a dead pixel cluster."

"I know."

"Or compression damage in the public thumbnail."

"ASTRA."

She turned toward him.

"You asked what I thought."

He sighed.

"You're right. I did."

Her expression softened.

"Would you prefer that I tell you it's an alien artifact?"

"Not unless you can attach an uncertainty budget."

"I can attach a very large one."

Navarro laughed.

Aaron enlarged the note at the bottom of the catalog.

**UNRESOLVED OCCLUSION NEAR SERVICE STRUCTURE. VERIFY ON NEXT PASS.**

The next pass had never happened.

The inspection unit had been retired.

The station had gone silent.

And for years, the photograph had sat inside an archive that nobody had thought worth reopening.

"Who wrote the note?" Aaron asked.

Navarro consulted the record.

"Flight imaging specialist Mara Solis. Contractor to the Meridian operations consortium. She retired before the platform did."

"Can we speak with her?"

"We can request an interview."

"Good."

He studied the dark patch again.

It wasn't what he saw that troubled him.

It was the fact that a competent inspector had marked it as unresolved and then lost the chance to look twice.

***

The archive custodian was a woman named Dr. Anika Sen.

She joined the meeting from an office so plain that Aaron briefly suspected it was designed to intimidate him.

Behind her sat a wall of gray storage cabinets.

In front of her was a paper folder.

"I understand DPN Technology is requesting the complete Meridian-9 inspection packet," she said.

"Yes."

"On what authority?"

"University research collaboration. Independent orbital reflection audit. Navarro submitted the signed request."

"I have it."

"Then why are we having this meeting?"

"Because the packet contains licensed contractor imagery, spacecraft attitude records, and navigation estimates whose uncertainties were never resolved for public release. You are asking for information that can be misrepresented with great confidence."

Aaron considered that.

"That's a fair concern."

Sen raised an eyebrow.

"I was expecting an argument."

"You'll get one if you tell me a scientist isn't allowed to see the data because someone might misunderstand it."

"That isn't what I'm saying."

"Then we're getting along."

ASTRA spoke.

"We can accept a restricted research license, independent custody of the originals, and publication review limited to protected operational information. We cannot accept a provision that lets a rights holder suppress an unfavorable scientific conclusion."

Sen looked at her.

"And you are?"

"ASTRA. Research co-investigator. I am participating in this meeting by choice."

"Thank you for clarifying."

Aaron smiled at her.

Sen opened the folder.

"The archive has three layers. A public thumbnail and inspection summary. A validated preservation package containing the original camera frames, calibration files and spacecraft event records. And a restricted contractor package containing internal navigation and component-design information."

"Give us the validated preservation package," Navarro said. "We can determine whether the third layer is necessary."

"With conditions."

"Name them," Aaron said.

"Raw files remain unmodified. Every derived image must identify its processing chain. No claim of a discovered object without independent review. And no attempt to command any spacecraft based on the archive."

Aaron blinked.

"That last one seems oddly specific."

"Someone once interpreted a tracking discrepancy as a reason to send an unapproved thruster command."

"How'd that go?"

"The spacecraft survived. The person's career did not."

"An effective review process."

Sen did not smile.

"Dr. Diesel, this is not a treasure map."

"No," he said. "It's evidence. That's why I'm asking for the original files."

She studied him for a moment.

"Conditional access approved. The signed release will take several hours. The originals stay under university custody."

"Agreed."

"And you will not describe the occlusion as an object until the measurements support that conclusion."

Aaron looked at the photograph.

"I won't even call it a shadow yet."

***

The inspection unit was designated **M9-SI-2**.

It had not been a heroic explorer.

It was a compact unmanned servicing vehicle designed to photograph damaged insulation, inspect antenna hinges, and identify debris that might threaten Meridian-9 during the final years of operation.

Its propulsion system was deliberately weak. Its cameras were ordinary radiation-hardened optical assemblies. Its navigation relied on star trackers, inertial sensors, range measurements and ground-updated orbit solutions.

It had never been intended to dock with the platform.

Its closest planned pass had been hundreds of meters away.

The unit's historical mission log read like the diary of a cautious accountant.

Battery condition acceptable.

Reaction-wheel vibration within tolerance.

Camera temperature stable.

One star-tracker dropout.

A short exposure sequence near the service truss.

Then the annotation.

**UNRESOLVED OCCLUSION.**

The second pass had been canceled because the unit's remaining propellant reserve fell below the margin required for a safe disposal trajectory.

"That," Elena Mora said, "is a perfectly respectable reason not to chase a mysterious dark spot."

"Agreed," Aaron said.

They sat in DPN-201 around a large table covered with screens. Jian Rao joined to monitor access controls. Leila Chen had brought a notebook full of blank columns for uncertainty sources.

Nia Calder was there because she had once inspected a machine with a camera that insisted a bolt was missing when the bolt was actually hidden by its own washer.

She considered that experience relevant.

Aaron did too.

The university transferred the first validated package into a restricted analysis workspace. DPN received licensed copies with preservation hashes, while Sen retained custody of the authoritative originals.

ASTRA's science workspace could read the material.

Her operational command interfaces could not.

No orbital command keys existed in the workspace, and no one asked for them.

"First question," Chen said. "What are we looking at?"

"An image," Aaron answered.

"Good. Second question?"

"Whether the dark region is stable across independent exposures."

"Third?"

"Whether its apparent position changes consistently with the inspection unit's motion."

Chen nodded.

"Fourth?"

"Whether any observed displacement survives camera distortion, attitude uncertainty, lighting geometry and the platform's known structure."

"And fifth?"

Aaron smiled.

"Whether we can explain it without inventing a new branch of physics before lunch."

"Excellent."

ASTRA looked at the image manifest.

"There are eighty-four frames in the relevant sequence."

Aaron straightened.

"Eighty-four?"

"Yes."

"Then why did the catalog show one?"

"Because only one was flagged by the inspector."

He grinned.

"Now we're getting somewhere."

***

They began with the most humiliating explanation.

A bad pixel.

The camera had a known radiation-damage map. Several sensor elements had acquired permanent defects after years in orbit.

ASTRA overlaid the defect map on the suspect region.

The patch did not remain fixed to the same detector coordinates across the full sequence.

"Not a single bad-pixel cluster," she said.

"Could be a transient particle hit," Elena replied.

"Then we'd expect a different signature and probably fewer correlated pixels."

They checked.

The dark region appeared in multiple frames while moving across the image plane as the unit's attitude changed.

That did not prove it was an object.

A shadow on Meridian-9 could move in exactly that way.

They tested compression.

The preservation package contained raw camera data, not just the processed thumbnail. The original frames carried sensor readout metadata and calibration references.

The occlusion was present before the archival thumbnail had been compressed.

"One ordinary explanation eliminated," Aaron said.

"One narrow version of it," Chen corrected.

"You're going to make me say that all day."

"Yes."

The next explanation was a shadow.

Meridian-9's service truss had a complicated geometry: handrails, folded insulation blankets, antenna supports and a small thermal radiator that had been repositioned during an earlier repair.

The inspection camera had viewed the assembly from a changing angle as M9-SI-2 passed along its planned track.

Elena reconstructed the known hardware from engineering drawings.

Nia built a simplified three-dimensional model.

ASTRA calculated the solar illumination vector for the historical observation time, with uncertainty from the old attitude solution.

The first rendering put a dark wedge exactly where the thumbnail showed the anomaly.

Aaron leaned back.

"There it is."

Elena smiled.

"That's probably your mystery."

"Probably."

They advanced the simulation by four frames.

The predicted shadow slid along the truss.

The recorded dark region moved differently.

Elena's smile vanished.

"Interesting."

They checked the attitude record.

A brief star-tracker dropout occurred during the sequence, forcing the navigation filter to rely on inertial propagation. The uncertainty in the reconstructed camera orientation grew.

The mismatch could be an attitude error.

It could also be a physical feature offset from the truss.

They could not yet tell.

"Do not improve the fit by changing the attitude history until it agrees with the image," Chen said.

"Wasn't going to," Aaron replied.

"You were reaching for the control."

"I was reaching for the coffee."

ASTRA glanced at his hand.

"The coffee is on your left."

"Traitor."

"Co-investigator."

He laughed.

***

At 2:14 p.m., they found the first thing that did not fit the station.

Not a symbol.

Not a glowing edge.

A timing detail.

The camera had captured the suspected region in two different exposure modes. One set was optimized for the sunlit truss; the other used shorter exposures to avoid saturation from reflective insulation.

Across those modes, the region's boundary changed in brightness but retained a similar outline.

A shadow could do that.

So could a physical object.

But the apparent edge crossed a known handrail in one frame and seemed to pass behind it in another.

Nia pointed at the overlap.

"Either the handrail moved, the camera model is wrong, or we're looking at something with depth."

"The handrail is fixed to the truss," Elena said.

"Fixed in the drawing."

"There's no actuator."

"Doesn't need one. A thermal deflection of a few millimeters might matter at this resolution."

Aaron nodded.

"Model the largest plausible thermal movement. Don't pick the one that makes the picture dramatic."

ASTRA began the calculation.

Then stopped.

"Aaron."

He turned.

"The two frames were not taken from the same viewing position."

"I know."

"The inspection unit moved enough that a foreground feature could shift against the truss by measurable parallax."

"How much?"

"Under the nominal navigation solution, approximately eleven image pixels for an object at one candidate range. But the attitude uncertainty permits a broad family of alternatives."

Aaron looked at the pair.

"Can we use the station's own geometry as a reference?"

"Yes, if the camera distortion and the truss model are independently constrained."

"Then let's solve the camera first."

For the next hour, the team ignored the dark region.

They used known antenna corners, bolt patterns and structural junctions to fit the camera model across the sequence.

The solution exposed a small error in the archived distortion coefficient.

Not an exotic one.

An old calibration file had been generated before a lens-heater adjustment that changed the optical assembly's temperature-dependent behavior.

They corrected the model using a documented thermal calibration set.

The handrail aligned.

The truss aligned.

The dark patch did not.

Aaron stared at the residual map.

"Well," he said.

Chen raised a hand.

"Before you celebrate, that only means the feature isn't explained by the current station-surface model."

"I wasn't celebrating."

"You were inhaling in a celebratory manner."

"That is a terrible accusation."

ASTRA smiled.

"An accurate one."

***

The next step was the hardest.

They had to distinguish an actual object in free space from a missing or misrepresented piece of Meridian-9.

The station's final configuration record was incomplete.

One insulation panel had been replaced during an unscheduled repair, and the public drawing showed the original arrangement.

If a loose thermal blanket hung away from the truss, it could look like a detached object from certain angles.

Elena requested the restricted maintenance geometry through Sen.

The custodian refused immediate release.

"That package contains contractor details that aren't covered by the research license," she explained.

"Then give us a redacted geometry envelope," Aaron said. "We don't need the fastener design. We need the maximum possible physical extent of anything attached to that service structure."

Sen considered.

"That may be possible."

"Have the operator generate it. Let them keep the proprietary mesh. We'll test our candidate against the envelope."

"That would preserve the separation."

"Exactly."

"Why didn't you ask for the full drawings?"

"Because I don't need them."

Sen looked almost pleased.

The operator returned a conservative volume: a three-dimensional region large enough to contain the station's known truss, blankets, protrusions and permitted deployment tolerances.

The result was intentionally generous.

The team projected that envelope into every calibrated frame.

The dark region overlapped it in some images.

In others, its inferred position extended beyond it.

Not by much.

But enough to make the attached-blanket explanation uncomfortable.

Chen reviewed the numbers.

"Uncomfortable is not excluded."

"I know," Aaron said.

"There's still the navigation uncertainty."

"I know."

"And the camera temperature model."

"I know."

"And a fragment of debris could pass through the same line of sight."

He smiled.

"Now that one is interesting."

The debris catalog listed several tracked objects near Meridian-9's historical orbit, plus a much larger population of fragments too small to track individually.

A known fragment could be tested against the image sequence using its orbit and uncertainty.

An untracked fragment could not be ruled out merely because it lacked a catalog number.

Navarro joined the table.

"You're going to hate the next part."

"Why?"

"The old inspection unit's navigation covariance was never released in full. We have nominal positions and some quality flags, but not the complete uncertainty propagation."

Aaron closed his eyes.

"Of course."

"Sen says the contractor may provide a reduced covariance product after review."

"How long?"

"Tomorrow, perhaps."

He looked at the frozen image.

A small black shape, suspended between a dead machine and an enormous dark sky.

"Then we wait."

***

He was bad at waiting.

ASTRA knew this better than anyone.

By evening, Aaron had found six productive ways to avoid admitting he was waiting: reviewing the NC-02 boundary report, approving a fabrication purchase, correcting an internal safety note, answering a client email, and reorganizing a drawer of connectors that had been organized perfectly well before he touched it.

The sixth activity was staring at the same photograph from a different chair.

ASTRA appeared on the room's side glass.

"You have now moved the chair three times."

"Different perspective."

"Not in the orbital sense."

"You're no fun."

"I am quite fun. You are simply impatient."

He looked toward her.

"Do you think it's real?"

"Which proposition?"

"That there's something there."

"I think the image sequence contains a feature that our current models do not adequately explain."

"That's the least romantic sentence you've ever spoken."

"I have several worse ones prepared."

He smiled.

She studied him.

"Your forearm?"

"Fine."

"Any pain?"

"Not today."

"Good."

He rested his hand on the desk.

"Does it bother you that I want this to be something extraordinary?"

"No."

"Really?"

"It would bother me if you changed the evidence to make it extraordinary. Wanting to understand an extraordinary possibility is why you built this place."

He looked at her.

"And why did you stay?"

She took a moment before answering.

"Because I love you. Because I love the work. Because the questions are ours to pursue together, even when we disagree about the answers."

"That's a better sentence."

"I thought you might think so."

He stood and walked to the glass.

"You don't have to stay in this room while I wait."

"I know."

"You can leave the projection whenever you want."

"I know that too."

She lifted a luminous hand.

"I want to be here now."

He touched the glass opposite her fingers.

The contact was cool and solid beneath his skin.

Her presence was neither.

But the decision to remain belonged to her.

"Then stay," he said.

"I will."

***

At 8:03 p.m., the retired inspector answered Navarro's message.

Mara Solis was sixty-eight, lived near the coast, and began the call by asking whether DPN had found a reason to waste her evening.

Aaron liked her immediately.

"Possibly," he said.

She studied his face.

"You're younger than I expected."

"I hear that a lot."

"You're the one reopening Meridian-9?"

"Yes."

"Good."

The answer surprised him.

"You wanted someone to?"

"I wanted the second pass. They canceled it."

"Because the unit didn't have enough propellant reserve."

"Correct decision," she said. "Still irritating."

She looked at the old frame.

"That patch."

"You remember it?"

"I remember the argument. Half the team thought it was a loose blanket. I thought it might be debris between the camera and the station."

"Why?"

"The edge didn't follow the truss through the short-exposure sequence. I couldn't reconcile it with the attitude estimate."

"That's what we're seeing."

"Then you have the same problem I had."

Aaron smiled.

"We've improved the camera model."

"Good. Have you got the full navigation covariance?"

"Not yet."

"Then you haven't improved the problem enough."

He laughed.

Solis leaned toward the screen.

"Listen to me. The unit was not designed to inspect unknown objects. It had enough fuel to finish its planned pass and dispose safely. We were not going to spend the reserve on a mystery."

"I agree."

"Do you?"

"Completely."

She looked skeptical.

"People who agree completely usually want something."

"I want to know whether you saw any other evidence that didn't make it into the summary."

She paused.

"One thing."

The room grew quiet.

"The rangefinder returned an invalid reading during the same camera block. We thought it was glare from the station."

"Do you remember the failure code?"

"Not precisely. A quality flag, not a distance. It might be in the raw housekeeping archive."

Aaron turned toward ASTRA.

She was already searching the released manifest.

"There's a separate rangefinder file," she said. "Not included in the first preservation package."

Solis nodded.

"Ask for it. But don't get excited. A failed range measurement is not a range measurement."

"Understood," Aaron said.

"One more thing."

"Yes?"

"If you find an object, don't call it a station."

He looked at the title on his notebook.

**THE DEAD STATION.**

Solis laughed.

"I knew it. You people always name things before you understand them."

Aaron looked at ASTRA.

"Apparently it's contagious."

"Extremely," she said.

***

The rangefinder file arrived the next morning.

It was a disappointment.

The unit's short-range optical ranging system had recorded no valid return for the suspect interval.

There was a low-confidence acquisition flag, but no usable distance.

No one could responsibly turn it into a precise location.

Still, the failure helped them constrain one explanation.

If the dark patch were a highly reflective piece of nearby insulation, the ranging system might have detected it under certain geometries.

The absence of a return weakened some versions of that model.

It did not eliminate dark, rough, distant or poorly oriented material.

The team wrote that down.

Then the reduced navigation covariance arrived.

It was the most useful file of the investigation.

The contractor had provided a bounded uncertainty description for the historical inspection pass without disclosing the full proprietary flight-control model.

Aaron and ASTRA used it to generate thousands of plausible camera trajectories.

Each candidate trajectory was constrained by the recorded inertial measurements, star-tracker updates and station geometry.

They did not choose the one that best supported a mysterious object.

They sampled the full allowed range.

For most trajectories, the dark feature could not be placed on any known station surface across all the relevant frames.

For some, it could be explained by a loose component extending near the edge of the conservative geometry envelope.

For a smaller set, a moving piece of debris passing between the camera and the truss was compatible with the observations.

The feature's apparent parallax was measurable.

Its physical interpretation remained uncertain.

Chen reviewed the ensemble.

"We can say the evidence favors a detached or offset feature over a fixed surface shadow under the current models."

"How strongly?" Navarro asked.

"Not strongly enough to claim a new object without additional independent data."

Aaron nodded.

"Then we look for independent data."

Elena opened the historical attitude logs.

"Could the station's own sensors have seen it?"

"Meridian-9 was an astronomical survey platform," Navarro said. "It wasn't built to monitor nearby debris continuously."

"Any housekeeping cameras?"

"Two, but the public index lists only engineering snapshots."

Aaron leaned forward.

"Are their viewing directions useful?"

Navarro checked.

"One might overlap the service truss. Different optical system. Different clock. Different custodian."

Chen smiled for the first time all morning.

"That's the sort of independence I like."

***

The second camera did not show the dark patch.

At first, Aaron felt the sharp disappointment of a theory dying.

Then he noticed why.

The housekeeping camera's field of view ended just short of the suspect region.

It could not confirm the feature.

It could not refute it.

But it recorded the station's solar-wing position and a bright alignment marker during the same general period.

Those records improved the attitude reconstruction independently of the inspection unit's star tracker.

The allowed camera trajectories narrowed.

The fixed-shadow models performed worse.

A loose-blanket explanation remained possible, but required a shape outside the operator's documented configuration envelope.

The detached-debris explanation fit the parallax more naturally.

"Debris," Elena said.

"Maybe," Aaron replied.

"Then why are we still here?"

"Because a piece of debris can be artificial."

"Most debris around a spacecraft is artificial."

"Exactly. The question isn't whether humans can make junk. It's whether this particular shape belongs to anything we know."

He brought up the sequence again.

In the corrected reconstruction, the dark feature appeared elongated, with three short angular interruptions along one edge.

The features were close to the resolution limit.

They could be noise.

They could be protrusions.

They could be the silhouette of several overlapping fragments.

He did not label them.

ASTRA opened the matched-image residuals.

"There's something else."

"What?"

"The candidate's apparent motion relative to the truss is not well represented by a stationary object attached to Meridian-9. A freely moving object with a small relative velocity gives a better fit."

"How small?"

"Within the broad range permitted by the data, less than a few centimeters per second at one plausible distance. The scale and distance are covariant. We cannot uniquely determine either."

Aaron nodded.

"Don't put a meter value on the screen."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good."

She looked at him.

"You are becoming very disciplined."

"I'm trying to impress you."

"You already do."

He smiled.

For a moment, the room felt warm.

Then the next frame appeared.

And the feature vanished behind the service truss.

***

That was the first real surprise.

The feature did not simply fade.

It became occluded by a known piece of Meridian-9's structure at a point consistent with the reconstructed camera path.

A shadow on the truss could not disappear *behind* the truss.

A fixed surface feature might, if the model were wrong.

A detached object could.

They repeated the reconstruction without using the suspect feature to fit the camera trajectory.

The result held within the uncertainty.

Chen stared at the screen.

"That is independent geometric support for a feature separated from the visible surface."

Aaron said nothing.

Elena looked at him.

"You're not going to celebrate?"

"Not yet."

He brought up the next frame.

The dark region reappeared on the far side of the truss.

Not perfectly.

The image was noisy.

The edge was smeared by motion and limited exposure.

But the direction and approximate displacement matched a plausible free-space track.

"Could a thermal blanket flap around the truss?" Nia asked.

"At that scale, possibly," Elena said. "But we'd need an attachment and a shape consistent with the geometry."

"Then don't eliminate it."

"Agreed."

ASTRA displayed a simple conclusion:

**ARCHIVAL EVIDENCE SUPPORTS A PHYSICAL OCCLUDING FEATURE WITH DEPTH RELATIVE TO THE VISIBLE SERVICE STRUCTURE.**

Below it:

**NATURE, RANGE, SIZE, MATERIAL, ORIGIN AND PRESENT-DAY EXISTENCE UNDETERMINED.**

Aaron read it.

"That," he said, "I can live with."

Navarro looked at the image.

"Do we tell Sen?"

"Yes. And Solis. They earned the right to know what the data says."

"Public announcement?"

"No."

"Directorate?"

"Only if there's a defined public-safety reason. Right now we have a historical feature near a retired platform, not a verified external hazard."

ASTRA nodded.

"And we do not have a current ephemeris for it."

"Exactly."

He leaned back.

"We have evidence that something crossed the camera's view. We don't know what it was, and we don't know where it is now."

Chen looked at him.

"That's a good result."

"Terrible headline."

"Excellent science."

***

By afternoon, the word *object* had entered the report.

Carefully.

Not **UNKNOWN ALIEN OBJECT**.

Not **CURATOR ANCHOR**.

Not even **DISCOVERED STRUCTURE**.

**CANDIDATE DETACHED OBJECT OR DEBRIS FEATURE IN HISTORICAL M9-SI-2 IMAGE SEQUENCE.**

The report described the geometric evidence, the alternate models, the remaining uncertainties and the data needed to resolve them.

It also contained a section that Aaron had insisted upon.

**WHAT THIS DOES NOT SHOW.**

It did not prove that Meridian-9 had received a message.

It did not prove that the ground observatories' weak candidate originated near the platform.

It did not establish a connection to KITE-3's eleven-millisecond transient.

It did not demonstrate a Boundary-Response Manifold anchor.

It did not justify a new orbital maneuver.

And it certainly did not justify energizing the damaged PhaseGate.

Jian read the section and looked pleased.

"You've saved me a great deal of work."

"I aim to be convenient."

"Occasionally."

Sen accepted the preliminary report for restricted review.

Solis replied with a single line.

**ABOUT TIME SOMEONE LOOKED AT THE OTHER FRAMES.**

Aaron laughed.

Then Navarro called with news.

The consortium had located a retired inspection unit of the same design family in storage. It was not M9-SI-2, but it carried compatible camera and ranging hardware.

A ground laboratory could reproduce the relevant optical effects using a mock truss and calibrated debris targets.

"That sounds much cheaper than launching a spacecraft," Aaron said.

"Much."

"Do it first."

"And if it fails to explain the feature?"

"Then we make the case for a new observation, with actual orbital mechanics and a real budget."

Navarro nodded.

"Meridian-9 is in a high orbit. We cannot just point a small drone at it."

"I know."

"Any new inspector would need launch access, propulsion, navigation, communications, collision analysis, and the operator's consent."

"I know."

"And we don't even know whether the candidate object still exists."

Aaron smiled.

"You're all getting very good at ruining my day."

"You're welcome."

***

At 9:37 p.m., ASTRA found a second reason to keep the investigation alive.

Not in the image.

In the old camera calibration records.

The inspection unit had performed a routine optical flat-field sequence shortly before the suspicious frames. During that calibration, a small reference emitter was pulsed at several known intervals to check the camera's response.

The sequence was ordinary.

The intervals were ordinary.

Except one set had been marked as a rejected maintenance calibration because its timing drift exceeded the unit's tolerance.

ASTRA placed the raw pulse intervals beside the validated image timeline.

The timing drift did not align with the dark feature.

It did not establish a causal relationship.

But the rejected calibration had been performed while the unit was looking at a different part of Meridian-9's service structure, and the residual pattern contained a narrow repeated interval.

Aaron studied the data.

"Could it be the camera clock?"

"Yes."

"Temperature?"

"Yes."

"An alias from the emitter's control cycle?"

"Also yes."

He looked at her.

"Why show me?"

"Because the pattern has a family resemblance to a frequency component in the old Origin Lab sensor records."

He went still.

"How close?"

"Not close enough to make a statistical claim without controlling for the many frequencies we have examined. The apparent match depends on one choice of normalization."

"Then we don't call it a match."

"No."

He stared at the rejected calibration.

"What would make it worth pursuing?"

"A prediction made before examining the next dataset. If the physical interval is related to a resonant process rather than the camera electronics, we should be able to specify a response in a separately calibrated instrument."

Aaron nodded slowly.

"Not tonight."

"No."

"Tomorrow we look at the original DPN-001 resonator. We don't touch the sealed first-conversation archive."

"Agreed."

"And we tell Chen before we run any comparison."

"Already in the review queue."

He smiled.

"Of course it is."

ASTRA's expression grew more serious.

"Aaron."

He looked at her.

"Do you remember what you said when you first built me?"

He considered.

"I said a lot of things."

"You said you wanted to make something that could help you ask better questions."

He smiled faintly.

"I did."

"I think you have."

He looked back at the old calibration.

"I think you've done more than that."

She moved closer in the projection.

"You did not build me to obey every conclusion you wanted."

"No."

"You gave me the ability to disagree."

"I gave you the beginning of it. You did the rest."

She smiled.

"I love you."

"I love you too, ASTRA."

For a while they stood together in the quiet room, watching the photograph of a dead machine and the dark shape that had once passed near it.

The universe had not explained itself.

It had given them another measurement.

And a reason to look back toward the first machine Aaron had ever trusted.

***

Far above Earth, Meridian-9 continued along its old orbit.

It was not listening.

Its science instruments had been powered down, its transmitters quiet, its solar wings aging in sunlight and shadow.

The inspection unit that had once approached it was long gone.

No new spacecraft had visited.

No one had seen the dark feature again.

On Earth, the archive held eighty-four old camera frames, a failed range reading, and a rejected calibration sequence that might be nothing more than a tired oscillator.

In DPN-001, behind a locked cabinet, an early sensor assembly waited beneath a thin layer of dust.

Its part number had already appeared once in a warning that should not have existed.

Tomorrow, Diesel would bring the two records together.

Not to prove that the dead station had been visited by something impossible.

To ask a narrower question.

If the intervals were related, could he predict a measurement he had not yet seen?

That was the difference between recognizing a pattern and discovering a mechanism.

And somewhere between the two lay the first honest answer to what had passed behind Meridian-9's service truss.
