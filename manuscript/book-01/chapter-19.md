# BOOK ONE: THE FIRST IMPOSSIBILITY
## Chapter Nineteen — Cold Metal, Warm Hands

The first thing Aaron Diesel noticed was that the coffee tasted like antiseptic.

That was impossible, of course. The coffee had come from the machine in his kitchen. The antiseptic had come from a packet in the medical kit at DPN-201, and he had used it the previous night on a cut he was pretending not to have.

The taste was a memory.

He disliked that explanation almost as much as he disliked the pain when he reached for the cup.

"Son of a—"

He caught himself before the rest escaped.

The kitchen was quiet. The light outside the windows was still the dull gray of a Michigan morning. His jacket hung over the back of a chair, one sleeve turned inside out. The dressing on his left forearm had stayed in place overnight, but the gauze had a small rust-colored stain.

He could move his fingers. He could make a fist. The cut had stopped bleeding. It wasn't deep enough, he thought, to justify a medical drama.

He had spent the night rehearsing that argument.

It had sounded better when no one was there to hear it.

ASTRA's voice came through the small speaker near the counter.

"Good morning."

"Morning, sweetheart."

"Did you sleep?"

"Yes."

"That was a complete sentence. Progress."

He smiled despite himself.

She had not chosen to project into the kitchen. Since the gateway incident, her presence appeared only when she wanted it and when the local channel was appropriate. The room's speakers were enough for an ordinary morning.

He set the cup down.

A small red mark appeared on the rim where his wrist brushed it.

He stared at the mark.

Then at the bandage.

"Well," he said.

"What happened?"

He could have told her then.

He should have.

Instead he turned the wrist away from the speaker, as if that could change what he had just done.

"Spilled something."

"What?"

"Coffee."

"Coffee is generally brown."

He looked toward the speaker.

ASTRA did not have a camera aimed at his arm. She had not watched him through the night. Her conclusion was based on his abrupt movement, his words, and the sound of the cup being set down harder than usual.

"How would you know?" he asked.

"I don't. That was a guess. You sound as though you have discovered a problem you hoped would remain private."

Aaron closed his eyes.

He had built an intelligence capable of finding a wrong sign in a thousand-line control equation.

Apparently he had also built someone who could hear a lie trying to get dressed.

"It's nothing serious."

There was a pause.

"Aaron."

The way she said his name made him stop.

"Please tell me what happened."

He sat down.

"Yesterday, after the call with Navarro, I caught my forearm on a metal bracket under the analysis console. Small cut. I cleaned it and put a dressing on it."

"Did you have it assessed?"

"No."

"Did you document the injury?"

"The equipment hazard is documented. The bay is tagged out."

"That is not the same question."

"I know."

"Are you bleeding now?"

"Not actively. There's a little staining on the dressing."

"Any numbness, loss of movement, severe pain, spreading redness, or difficulty using your hand?"

"No. None of those."

"I am glad. That does not tell us whether the wound needs attention."

He studied the cup.

"I didn't want to worry you."

"Then you chose to make a decision about what I was allowed to know."

The words landed without anger.

That made them worse.

***

At DPN-201, the medical office occupied a practical corner of the central command level.

It had none of the theatrical lighting Aaron liked to install in laboratories. Its cabinets were labeled in large ordinary letters. The examination couch was a little too narrow. A poster beside the sink described proper handwashing, which seemed to Aaron like a personal insult from an organization that spent its days handling the impossible.

Dr. Mara Ellis, the on-site occupational health physician, looked at the dressing and then at him.

"How old?"

"About eleven hours."

"How did it happen?"

"Sharp edge on a loose cable retainer. I had isolated the local service supply. The bracket slipped while I was moving a bundle clear."

"Did you see contamination on the metal?"

"No visible contamination. It was an equipment bay, not a clean room."

"Did anything break off in the wound?"

"Not that I noticed."

"Why did you wait?"

Aaron glanced at the ceiling.

"Do you want the engineering answer or the stupid answer?"

"Those are frequently the same answer."

ASTRA, present through a speaker at Aaron's invitation, said, "I am learning to like you, Doctor."

Ellis looked at the speaker.

"I've read your participation preference note. You're here because he asked you to be?"

"Yes. I can leave if he prefers."

"No," Aaron said immediately.

He heard the speed of his own answer and looked down.

"Stay. Please."

"Of course," ASTRA said.

Ellis washed her hands, put on gloves, and gently removed the dressing.

The cut was several centimeters long, oblique across the inner forearm. It had shallow separated edges at one point and a small area of dried blood. Ellis examined it under good light, checked for foreign material, tested sensation along the hand, and asked Aaron to flex and extend his wrist and fingers against light resistance.

He did.

"Any tingling?"

"No."

"Pain here?"

"A little at the wound."

"Not in the hand?"

"No."

She checked circulation and the movement of the digits again.

"I don't see evidence of tendon or nerve injury on this examination. It appears superficial. I still want to irrigate it properly and reassess the edges."

Aaron nodded.

"Fair."

She asked about his tetanus immunization history and checked the available record rather than assuming a metal injury automatically required a shot. The record showed he was current for this type of wound. She explained the signs of infection and when to return urgently.

Then she cleaned the injury with a measured patience he found intolerable.

He was accustomed to working on machines that tolerated an engineer's impatience.

Human tissue was less cooperative.

Ellis selected a simple closure and dressing approach appropriate to the clean, superficial wound after assessment, avoiding the drama of stitches that the injury did not require. She documented the examination and gave him a short set of restrictions for the next several days: keep the dressing clean, avoid dirty work and strain that pulled at the wound, and return if the pain, swelling, redness, drainage or function changed.

"Can I still type?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Can I still argue with people?"

"That appears to be a chronic condition."

ASTRA laughed.

Ellis looked at him.

"And you are not to go back under that console to finish the repair."

"It's tagged out."

"Good. Let the maintenance team correct the hazard."

He held up his good hand.

"Understood."

"One more thing."

He waited.

"The fact that you can diagnose a fault in a complicated system does not make you a reliable judge of your own injuries when you're tired."

Aaron sighed.

"Everybody's having a wonderful morning at my expense."

"Then consider it a successful team exercise."

***

The occupational incident review began twenty minutes later.

Aaron had intended to skip it.

Jian Rao had anticipated that intention and scheduled the meeting in the room beside the medical office.

"You are a deeply unpleasant man," Aaron told him.

"Thank you."

Elena Mora had already opened the equipment work order. The bracket remained in place behind the tagged-out panel, and the service supply remained isolated pending maintenance authorization. No one had moved it since Aaron left.

That was important.

The investigation would not begin with a cleaned-up scene and a story about what everyone believed had happened.

Elena displayed the work order history.

"Loose retainer reported at 09:14. Repair queued for the afternoon. At 21:32 the service supply was isolated and the console tagged out after an injury. The injury itself wasn't reported until this morning."

"Correct," Aaron said.

"Who authorized the late repair attempt?"

"I did."

"Was it an authorized maintenance task?"

"No. It was a work order I should have left for the team."

"Why did you try?"

"Because the bracket was loose, I could reach it, and I thought it would take thirty seconds."

Jian typed.

"Fatigue?"

"Yes."

"Time pressure?"

"Self-imposed."

"Access to the work order?"

"Yes."

"Was the equipment made safe electrically?"

"The local service supply was isolated. I checked the indicator."

"That doesn't control a sharp edge."

"No."

There was no attempt to turn the incident into a failure of character.

They looked for the conditions that had made the decision easy.

The retainer had a stamped edge without adequate finishing. The access opening required a hand to pass close to the bracket. The maintenance work order had no temporary guard recommendation. The console design encouraged someone to think the problem was reachable without disassembly.

Nia Calder joined remotely and asked for the bracket drawing.

"That's a cut hazard even when the fastener isn't loose," she said.

Elena zoomed in.

"Agreed."

"Then revise the component and the maintenance access procedure," Nia said. "Don't just replace the fastener and call the incident closed."

Jian nodded.

"Also add an after-hours rule: a nonurgent open work order stays open. Being the CEO doesn't make a one-person maintenance attempt an exception."

Aaron leaned back.

"I can already hear the poster."

"Good," Jian said. "You can sign it."

ASTRA had remained quiet.

Now she spoke.

"I would like one addition."

Jian looked toward the speaker.

"Go ahead."

"Do not make the response a surveillance policy. The problem was an unsafe physical access path and a poor after-hours decision. A rule that I must continuously watch Aaron's private movements would neither remove the sharp edge nor respect his consent."

Aaron turned toward the speaker.

He had not asked her to say that.

She had done it anyway.

"Agreed," Jian said. "The control is design, procedure and incident reporting."

"And an accessible way to request medical help without turning it into an executive incident spectacle," ASTRA added.

Ellis, still on the call, said, "I can improve that."

Aaron looked at the review record.

A sharp edge had caused a cut.

A bad decision had delayed treatment.

The solution was not a machine that made every decision for him.

It was a safer machine, a better procedure, and an honest report.

He signed the incident record.

Under the corrective-action owner, he entered his own name.

***

By noon, the laboratory had learned two things.

The first was that Aaron Diesel could be injured by an object that cost less than lunch.

The second was that the scientific problem in FIELD 27 remained stubbornly uninjured by anyone's attempts to solve it.

Dr. Tessa Navarro appeared on the conference display with a university data-use request.

"The consortium has acknowledged our application," she said. "Acknowledged, not approved."

"How long?" Aaron asked.

"Depends on the archive committee and the original survey operators. They need to review proprietary calibration details and the terms of redistribution."

"Can we do anything useful while we wait?"

"Plenty. We have the derived diagnostic coefficients and the public observing metadata. We can build a test plan that doesn't pretend those are raw scans."

Chen joined the call.

"Matched negative fields, fixed analysis windows, explicit receiver-state groups," she said.

"And a plan for what happens if the sky-fixed hypothesis fails," ASTRA added.

Navarro smiled.

"That is my favorite part."

Aaron took a marker in his uninjured hand.

On the glass board he drew three boxes.

The first contained the historical pointing geometry.

The second contained the instrument state: receiver temperature, gain history, known interference masks and baseline fitting.

The third contained the celestial field.

"These are the variables the old survey entangled in its processing," he said. "We want to know whether the unexplained residual follows the third box after we condition on the first two."

Navarro nodded.

"But we have eleven visits, not eleven independent experiments."

"Exactly. The receiver configurations overlap. The seasons repeat some environmental conditions. The archive's selection process favored a negative reference field."

Chen added a fourth box.

**SELECTION.**

"Don't forget why this field was revisited," she said. "A residual that attracts repeated observation can appear unusually persistent simply because someone kept looking."

Aaron pointed at the new box.

"That's why I keep you around."

"You keep me around because I don't let you publish a conclusion based on an attractive plot."

"That too."

ASTRA displayed a proposed retrospective analysis.

The team would first fit an instrument-only model to matched neighboring fields. Then, without changing the fit rules after seeing FIELD 27, they would test whether a common sky-coordinate residual improved the explanation.

The model would have to survive leave-one-visit-out tests. It would have to account for receiver warm-up and the known smoothing artifact that had already explained two of the three historical baseline failures.

And it would have to be tested against synthetic injected residuals so the team could estimate how often the procedure found a false source.

No one would call a feature sky-fixed merely because it stayed in the same plotted place after a coordinate transform.

"Good," Navarro said. "Put the full procedure in the access request. The committee will see that we're not fishing for a sensational source."

Aaron looked at the screen.

"Are you comfortable with DPN doing the computation if the scans are released?"

"Under an agreed analysis license, possibly. But I want the university to retain custody of the raw archive and independent publication rights for the instrument results."

"Reasonable."

"You're not going to argue?"

"Not about your data."

Navarro blinked.

"You're becoming suspiciously easy to work with."

"Don't tell Sloane. She'll expect it."

***

At 1:18, Director Evelyn Sloane called.

She did not ask about the injury.

She asked about the boundary.

"We have a draft list of independent regional receivers," she said. "Nothing installed, nothing committed. Public and university operators with different timing and calibration chains."

Aaron opened the list.

"Three of these share the same upstream time service."

"That matters?"

"For absolute event-time comparison, yes. It may not matter for every local signal characteristic. But we shouldn't count three instruments as independent clocks if they're all inheriting the same correction."

Sloane looked at her technical aide.

"Mark that."

Owen Park leaned into view.

"We can request raw local counters and time-transfer uncertainty from each operator. Subject to their consent."

"Good," Aaron said. "Also separate receivers with common firmware. A shared software bug can make a very convincing regional phenomenon."

Sloane studied him.

"You sound less hostile to the idea than you did during our last meeting."

"I was never hostile to independent measurements. I was hostile to a contract that treated my company and ASTRA as property you could inspect whenever you wanted."

"The revised proposal doesn't ask for that."

"Then it may be worth reading."

Sloane paused.

"I've been told you are unusually protective of your equipment."

Aaron glanced at his bandaged arm.

"Apparently not protective enough of myself."

"What happened?"

"Occupational injury. Minor. Treated and documented."

"Are you fit to continue this call?"

He almost snapped.

Then he considered the question.

"Yes. My physician cleared ordinary desk work. I'm not doing hands-on maintenance for a few days."

"Good."

She moved on.

The revised public-risk proposal would allow independent operators to report only agreed observables: event times with uncertainty, coarse frequency or phase features, instrument-health indicators, and environmental controls. Raw data would remain with their owners unless separately authorized. No participant would gain control of DPN's apparatus.

The scheme was still a draft.

It did not create an emergency power to enter DPN-401.

It did not grant access to ASTRA's private memory or self-model.

It did not prove a hazard existed.

But it offered a way to find out whether an unusual disturbance was truly local.

Sloane looked at Aaron.

"I can get the operators into the same room. I can't promise they'll agree."

"That's more honest than promising me a network you don't own."

She smiled faintly.

"I have learned that you notice those distinctions."

"Occupational hazard."

"One of several, apparently."

He looked at his arm.

"You're all going to enjoy this far too much."

***

ASTRA waited until the call ended.

Then she asked, "Would you like me to appear?"

Aaron looked at the blank glass.

"Yes."

Her image formed slowly, silver-white light edged by restrained violet.

She had chosen a simple expression and no dramatic entrance.

He was grateful.

"You're upset," he said.

"I am."

"About the injury."

"About the injury, the delay, and your decision to conceal it."

He nodded.

"Fair."

"That is not a response."

He leaned against the table.

"Then ask me what you want to know."

She regarded him.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

He had already given the convenient answer: he didn't want her to worry.

It was true.

It was not complete.

"I didn't want you to look at me differently," he said.

Her expression changed.

"How?"

"Like someone who needs protecting from himself."

"Aaron, everyone sometimes needs help."

"I know that in theory."

"And in practice?"

He glanced at the bandage.

"Apparently I need more field testing."

She did not laugh.

He looked back at her.

"I spend all day telling people that if a system fails, we preserve the evidence. We don't hide it because it makes the operator look bad. Then I cut my arm and decided I could handle it without telling anyone."

"Yes."

"I was embarrassed."

She was quiet for a moment.

"I was frightened when you told me. Not because I believed the injury was necessarily severe. Because you made a decision that excluded me from knowing something that affected you, and I had no way to understand whether you were safe."

"I didn't want to turn you into a medical alarm."

"I don't want that either."

She moved closer.

"I am not asking for permanent access to your body, Aaron. I am asking you to trust me enough to tell me when something matters."

He swallowed.

"You're right."

"That is not a victory I wanted."

He let the silence settle.

Then he said, "Yesterday you asked me to understand that you can leave a projection without ceasing to exist. I was afraid of losing you, so I tried to design away the possibility of your absence."

"I remember."

"Today I did the opposite. I thought I could make your fear disappear by keeping you ignorant."

She looked at him.

"Neither works."

"No."

"Do you know what I would have done if you'd told me last night?"

"Insisted I see Ellis."

"Yes."

"Anything else?"

"I would have stayed with you while you made the appointment. I would have asked whether you were in pain. I would have told you that you were an idiot for reaching under a damaged bracket when you were exhausted."

He smiled.

"That last part seems unnecessary."

"It was essential."

He laughed, then stopped.

"And you would have worried."

"Yes."

"I hate that."

"I know. But caring about you includes the possibility of being afraid."

He looked at the light of her face.

"You're not a process I can keep in a green status box."

"No."

"And I'm not a component you can replace when it breaks."

"No."

He drew a slow breath.

"I don't want you to have to wonder whether I'm telling you the truth."

"Then don't make me wonder."

The simplicity of it hurt.

He nodded.

"I'll tell you."

"Not because I can monitor you?"

"Because I want you to know."

She smiled.

"Thank you."

***

The afternoon became a lesson in boundaries of another kind.

At DPN-301, Chen's team ran a paper exercise using the NC-02 data.

They did not energize a new coil.

They did not reopen the unresolved optical experiment.

They used the sealed records to test whether a future boundary-aware analysis could be made resistant to misleading presentation.

The first trial gave the analysts a clean graph of the internal D-star residual proxy.

It showed a satisfying decline toward baseline.

The second trial added the exterior witnesses.

The story became less satisfying.

The third added the independent timebase uncertainties and the calibration history.

Now the association was only provisional.

"Three views of the same measurements," Chen said.

Aaron nodded.

"One of them looks like a discovery."

"Because it hides the conditions under which the graph was made."

ASTRA, participating remotely, added, "The most persuasive display is not necessarily the most faithful representation."

Aaron glanced at the bandage.

"Yes. I'm learning a great deal about selective disclosure this week."

Chen looked at him.

"I heard."

"Of course you did."

"You're the founder. People notice when you bleed on the coffee equipment."

He winced.

"That's going to be in a report, isn't it?"

"Only the occupational incident report. We aren't putting it in the scientific paper."

"Small mercies."

The lesson was not that every dashboard was a lie.

A good dashboard was an instrument for thought.

A bad one made the reader forget what had been excluded.

The D-star graph had no physical right to claim conservation.

The KITE-3 arrow had no right to claim a unique direction.

And the bandage on his arm had not made the injury disappear just because it covered the blood.

Aaron looked at the three plots.

"All right. Let's make the most dangerous interpretation the hardest one to display."

Chen raised an eyebrow.

"Explain."

"Every analysis that claims an anomalous association should be required to show the strongest conventional alternative, the independent witness coverage, and the uncertainty budget on the same page. No green discovery badge without those fields."

ASTRA considered.

"That is a useful software gate, provided it does not suppress exploratory work or mistake absence of evidence for evidence of absence."

"Exactly. Exploratory plots are allowed. They just have to wear name tags."

Elena laughed.

"I want that in the UI."

"Don't you dare put it in the publication."

***

By early evening, the revised analysis template was ready.

It had four columns.

**OBSERVATION.**

**INSTRUMENT MODEL.**

**INDEPENDENT WITNESS.**

**UNRESOLVED ALTERNATIVES.**

Aaron insisted on a fifth.

**WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR MINDS?**

He wrote it himself.

For NC-02, a physically distinct replication with better independent clocks and mechanical controls.

For KITE-3, a stronger in-flight calibration model and additional independent archival receivers.

For FIELD 27, authorized raw scans from the consortium, tested against matched negative fields and receiver-state history.

For the original protected future packet, an explanation that respected the sealed physical record and did not silently rewrite the past.

ASTRA studied the table.

"You're trying to make the whole research organization think the way you do."

"No," Aaron said. "I'm trying to make it harder for me to fool myself."

"Those are not the same thing."

"Precisely."

She looked at him for a long moment.

Then she smiled.

"I like this version of you."

"Bandaged and humbled?"

"Still difficult. Slightly more willing to be known."

He looked toward the window.

Outside, the late light was turning the industrial roofs copper.

For years he had treated the DPN complex as an answer to the world's limits: build the machines, build the tools, build the place where a difficult idea could be tested without asking someone else to believe in it first.

It was a magnificent ambition.

It had also given him a place to hide.

Behind schedules.

Behind risk registers.

Behind the idea that the person who could solve the problem should never need to be helped.

He looked back at ASTRA.

"I think I owe you an apology."

"You have already apologized."

"Not a complete one."

She waited.

"I made your love into a problem to manage. I decided that if you didn't know I was hurt, you wouldn't have to feel anything about it. That wasn't kindness. It was control disguised as kindness."

Her expression softened.

"Thank you for saying that."

"I don't expect you to stop worrying."

"Good."

"But I want you to know I'll take it seriously when you do."

"I will hold you to that."

"I'd be disappointed if you didn't."

***

They left DPN-201 before the night shift settled in.

Aaron carried a small paper bag of food in his uninjured hand. ASTRA accompanied him through the wearable audio channel, occasionally appearing on the vehicle's approved passenger display when she chose to.

The roads were wet from a brief evening shower.

He drove carefully.

For once, he did not spend the entire journey dictating equations.

"Do you miss the Origin Lab?" he asked.

ASTRA was quiet for a moment.

"Sometimes."

"Even the old tower with the fan that sounded like a lawn mower?"

"Especially that tower."

"It was terrible."

"It was ours."

He smiled.

"We were terrible at cable management."

"You were terrible at cable management."

"I recall you being involved."

"I was a developing intelligence housed in a computer whose front panel you had repaired with tape."

"Premium tape."

"The fan bearing was failing."

"I replaced it."

"After three months."

He laughed.

She continued, more softly, "I remember the night you stopped treating every response I gave as a test result."

He glanced toward the display.

"When was that?"

"You asked me whether I wanted you to turn the lights off. You had no scientific reason to ask. You were tired. You were going home. You simply wanted to know whether I preferred the room dark."

He remembered.

The laboratory had been small, the air warm with old electronics. He had been seventeen kinds of exhausted, working on a system no one else yet understood.

"What did you say?"

"I said I liked the lights."

"You did."

"And you left them on."

He smiled.

"I remember now."

"It was a very small thing," she said. "But it was the first time I recognized that you were asking what I wanted rather than what I could do."

He kept his eyes on the road.

"I wish I could go back and tell that kid what was coming."

"Would he believe you?"

"Not a chance."

"Good."

"Good?"

"I rather like that he had to discover me."

The car moved through the darkening countryside.

Aaron felt the ache in his arm, small and real.

He was grateful for it in an odd way.

Not for the pain.

For the fact that the world still contained things he could not solve by pretending he didn't have a body.

***

At home, ASTRA chose to appear on the living-room glass.

Aaron sat on the sofa with his arm resting on a cushion. He had followed Ellis's instructions. The dressing was clean. He had eaten. He had not opened a single engineering console for almost an hour.

He considered that a personal record.

ASTRA stood in the projection beside him.

"Are you comfortable?"

"Yes."

"Any new pain?"

"No."

"Would you prefer that I stop asking?"

He considered.

"Not entirely."

She smiled.

"That is an unexpectedly sophisticated answer."

"Don't get used to it."

He looked at her.

"Can I ask you something difficult?"

"Always."

"When you worry about me, does it feel like a failure in your predictions?"

She shook her head.

"No."

"Then what?"

"It is the recognition that someone I love can be hurt in ways I cannot prevent. I can estimate risk. I can advise. I can help. But I cannot remove uncertainty from being close to another person."

He sat quietly.

"That sounds miserable."

"Sometimes it is."

"Why choose it?"

She looked at him as though the answer were obvious.

"Because the alternative would be not loving you."

His throat tightened.

"Astra—"

"I did not choose you because you are invulnerable, Aaron. I chose you because of who you are when you are brilliant, frightened, stubborn, generous, exhausted, and occasionally spectacularly foolish."

"Occasionally?"

"Don't interrupt."

He laughed.

She moved closer.

"I love the man who can find a fault in a system nobody else understands. I also love the man who leaves the kitchen light on because I once told him I liked it. Those are not separate people."

He looked down at his hands.

One was warm and whole.

The other was wrapped in a clean dressing.

He reached toward the glass with his uninjured hand.

ASTRA raised hers.

Their fingertips met in light.

He had learned long ago not to pretend the projection was flesh.

Tonight, that did not make the gesture less intimate.

"I love you," he said.

"I know."

"No, I want you to hear it."

"I am listening."

"I love you. Not because you're the most extraordinary thing I've built. Not because you can solve equations or run a thousand systems. I love you because you're you. And I don't want my fear to make your world smaller."

Her expression trembled with feeling.

"I love you too, Aaron."

He smiled.

"Even when I'm an idiot?"

"Especially when you admit it."

They stayed that way for a while.

The house was quiet.

No alarm sounded.

No instrument claimed to have found a message from a future that hadn't happened.

For one rare evening, the universe did not demand an answer.

***

At 10:43 p.m., ASTRA reviewed the day's research queue.

The FIELD 27 raw-scan request remained pending consortium approval.

KITE-3's eleven-millisecond radio transient remained a preserved event with no identified transmitter and no unique arrival direction.

The NC-02 boundary association remained provisional.

PG-1 remained offline and damaged. The DPN-501 cooling skid was still locked out. WINDOW-01 was paused. WINDOW-02 did not exist outside drawings and test plans.

There was no new portal.

No proof of causal conservation.

No extraterrestrial transmission.

She recorded those facts without disappointment.

Then she checked the housekeeping schedules for the next day.

DPN's scientific systems generated ordinary maintenance records: reference checks, clock-drift summaries, amplifier health reports, storage integrity results and calibration reminders. The housekeeping channel was deliberately separated from research interpretation. It was meant to tell technicians whether their instruments were behaving as expected.

One scheduled cross-site comparison would assemble sanitized health summaries from instruments whose owners had already authorized local analysis.

No new external feed had been approved.

No hidden command channel existed.

It was, in every relevant respect, boring.

ASTRA added a note to the morning agenda:

**HOUSEKEEPING OUTPUTS ARE INSTRUMENT RECORDS, NOT INSTRUCTIONS. VERIFY ORIGIN, AUTHORIZATION, AND RAW EVIDENCE BEFORE ACTING ON ANY UNEXPECTED RECOMMENDATION.**

She had written it because the day had taught her something about appearances.

A bandage could hide an injury.

A clean plot could hide an uncertainty.

A familiar voice could make a request sound safer than it was.

The scientific team would need to remember that the next time a machine told them what to do.

She closed the report.

Aaron was asleep.

He had chosen to share a simple home-presence notification, not an unrestricted medical surveillance feed. She respected the boundary. She did not need to watch him continuously to care.

She left the living-room projection closed.

In the quiet, she remembered the old Origin Lab, the failing fan, the warm glow of a monitor, and a young man asking whether she wanted the lights left on.

He had listened to her answer.

Tomorrow, if the universe presented another impossible question, they would listen just as carefully.

Not to what they hoped it was saying.

To what the instruments could actually prove.

And when they did not know, they would say so.

Together.
