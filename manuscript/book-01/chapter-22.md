# BOOK ONE: THE FIRST IMPOSSIBILITY
## Chapter Twenty-Two — An Expensive Question

The first answer to Aaron Diesel's challenge came with an invoice.

Not from the unknown author of the mathematical warning.

From a woman named Dr. Miriam Voss, who directed the Harrow Ridge Observatory and had apparently decided that the laws of nature were less important than her institution's maintenance budget.

Her email was addressed to Dr. Tessa Navarro.

**WE CAN PROVIDE THE COMPARISON GEOMETRY.**

The next line was less encouraging.

**WE CANNOT PROVIDE IT FOR FREE.**

Aaron read the proposed price twice.

Then a third time, in case one of the zeros had been introduced by an especially cruel email client.

"Two hundred and forty-eight thousand dollars," he said.

ASTRA appeared on the glass beside his workstation.

"That is the estimated total project cost, not the observing-time charge."

"Thank you. I was afraid it was affordable."

"Would you like the breakdown?"

"Please. I want to know which component has learned to demand a salary."

She displayed a spreadsheet.

The observatory's unusual scheduling collision had freed an eighteen-hour comparison window in which two physically different receivers could observe overlapping portions of the same sky. One belonged to Harrow Ridge, a remote high-elevation station with a long-baseline interferometric receiver. The other was a smaller, independently operated instrument at Cairn Basin, several hundred kilometers away, built around a different front-end architecture and maintained by a different institution.

That was valuable.

It was also inconvenient.

The receivers did not share an absolute clock. Their frequency standards had different calibration histories. Harrow Ridge needed a new reference check before the window. Cairn Basin needed an overnight operator and an additional independent storage system. Both facilities needed engineers to document cable delays, receiver gain states, temperature behavior and local interference.

A clean comparison would require more than turning two dishes toward a patch of darkness.

The cost estimate included staff, instrument time, a transportable calibration package, independent data custody, secure transfer, insurance for the borrowed reference equipment, and a contingency reserve for weather or hardware failure.

Aaron pointed to a line item.

"Why are we paying someone to watch a receiver for eighteen hours?"

"Because an unattended receiver can fail in ways that look interesting," ASTRA said.

"That's an annoyingly good answer."

He examined the total again.

Two hundred and forty-eight thousand dollars.

He had once built a functioning server from parts that a recycling company had considered too ugly to sell.

Now he was contemplating a quarter-million-dollar question.

"Can we afford it?" he asked.

"That depends on which other obligations you are willing to defer."

He turned to her.

"Show me."

***

The DPN financial report was not a dramatic document.

There were no mysterious offshore accounts, no unlimited billionaire fortune and no secret government fund waiting to rescue the laboratory.

DPN Technology made money by building things that worked.

Industrial automation. Secure software. Manufacturing controls. Systems integration. Commercial infrastructure that required fewer philosophical arguments than spacetime research and usually generated an invoice someone actually paid.

Those contracts funded the experimental work.

The company had reserves, but they were not a decorative number.

Payroll was due. Suppliers expected payment. The DPN-401 safety remediation had already consumed a substantial portion of the year's research contingency. DPN-501's damaged cooling equipment remained isolated, and replacing it would not become cheaper merely because Aaron preferred looking at the stars.

ASTRA displayed the forecast.

"Funding the observatory allocation from unrestricted cash would not threaten immediate payroll," she said. "It would, however, reduce the reserve available for safety remediation and delay two planned manufacturing upgrades."

"How long?"

"Depending on client receivables, between six and eleven weeks."

"That sounds survivable."

"It is survivable. It is not costless."

He studied the cash-flow lines.

The old temptation was familiar.

If the science mattered enough, everything else could wait.

The trouble was that everything else consisted of real people who had agreed to build a company with him, not sacrifice their livelihoods to whatever experiment he found fascinating on a Thursday.

He closed the unrestricted-cash scenario.

"No."

ASTRA regarded him.

"No?"

"Not that way."

"You are allowed to reconsider."

"I am. I just did."

He opened the research expenditure ledger.

"Find the portion we can fund without touching payroll, emergency maintenance or committed client deliveries. Then show me what an external scientific partner could legitimately contribute without buying a piece of DPN."

ASTRA smiled.

"That is a different question."

"A more expensive one?"

"Possibly less expensive. Considerably more complicated."

"Excellent. Bureaucracy. My favorite branch of physics."

***

Dr. Voss joined the planning call from a control room with the exhausted dignity of a place that had survived decades of grant applications.

Behind her, a wall of monitors showed receiver temperatures, wind speed, tracking status and a long list of things that could go wrong without ever producing a scientific paper.

Navarro introduced everyone.

Aaron did not wait long.

"Your schedule says the overlap is available because of a maintenance conflict."

"Correct," Voss said. "The Harrow Ridge receiver was due for a calibration comparison. The work overlaps a planned service interruption. We can shift the service if we fund additional staff and complete the reference transfer in advance."

"And Cairn Basin?"

"Different operator. Different instrument. Their director has tentatively agreed to participate, subject to a signed protocol and their own safety approval."

"Who owns the data?"

"Each institution owns its raw records. A joint scientific analysis license can be negotiated."

"Who controls the receivers?"

"The observatories."

"Who decides when they shut down?"

"The observatories."

"Good."

Voss looked mildly surprised.

"Most private sponsors ask whether they can have priority access."

"I would like priority access to the truth. You can keep the antennas."

ASTRA appeared beside Aaron.

"Dr. Voss, we also require independent instrument-state records, not just processed spectra. The comparison will be meaningless if both sites use the same correction package without disclosing it."

Voss nodded.

"Agreed. Harrow Ridge uses a local acquisition clock and an external frequency reference. Cairn Basin uses a separate timing architecture. We can preserve the original sample counters, navigation-derived time estimates and the calibration steps."

"And no dashboard-aligned timestamps as proof of event order," Navarro added.

"Obviously," Voss said.

Aaron liked her immediately.

Then she ruined it.

"The proposal is not an eighteen-hour hunt for messages," she said. "It is a receiver-comparison campaign. If you want to search for a specific signal, you'll need to tell us what it is."

"We don't know what it is."

"Then you need a test that can fail."

"That's why we're calling."

Voss leaned back.

"All right, Doctor Diesel. Tell me what you want to falsify."

***

Aaron did not show her the private D-star coefficients.

He did not show her the protected PG-1 warning packet.

He did not describe the Curator as a person, a machine or an intelligence. Those were possibilities in a story he had not yet earned the right to tell.

Instead he shared the narrow public version of the challenge.

A published mathematical constraint, derived from an untrusted receiver maintenance artifact, appeared to make a conditional claim about discrepancies between independent boundary witnesses.

The challenge was not to obey that claim.

It was to see whether a prospective respondent could predict a measurable difference between two independently operated instruments under a precommitted set of conditions.

"Which conditions?" Voss asked.

"We choose them after the challenge digest is fixed," Aaron said. "The operator controls the schedule. We blind the exact timing and calibration states to the analysts who score candidate responses."

"And if no response arrives?"

"Then we still get a useful cross-site calibration dataset."

Voss studied the protocol.

"You're trying to make the experiment worthwhile even if your mysterious author doesn't exist."

"Precisely."

"That's sensible."

"Please don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

ASTRA added, "We will also register ordinary alternatives: local radio-frequency interference, atmospheric effects, receiver sidelobes, shared processing defects, timing corrections, orbital reflections and selection bias."

"Good," Voss said. "And you will not claim a direction from one station's software map."

Navarro smiled.

"We learned that lesson with KITE-3."

"Speaking of which," Voss said, "your proposal includes an orbital receiver."

Aaron looked at Navarro.

She took over.

"KITE-3 is a university spacecraft. Its existing science schedule is committed. We can request archived passive buffers and, perhaps, a routine acquisition mode during a naturally overlapping orbital pass. We cannot command a new high-rate observation without spacecraft operations approval, thermal and power assessment, and a downlink allocation."

"Can you buy a pass?" Voss asked.

"No," Navarro said. "You can request one. The spacecraft does not become a rental telescope because a sponsor has money."

"Good," Voss said. "Then don't put it in the required two-receiver success condition."

Aaron crossed the orbital leg out of the primary protocol.

He did not like it.

Which was one reason he knew it was right.

***

The next problem was geometry.

Two ground receivers separated by hundreds of kilometers could provide useful diversity, but only if the observing conditions were understood.

Harrow Ridge's horizon mask excluded part of the target region during the first half of the window. Cairn Basin could observe it longer, but with a different beam pattern and poorer low-frequency sensitivity.

The two stations did not point at exactly the same patch of sky with exactly the same response.

That was not a defect.

It was the reason the comparison could test whether a feature followed the sky, the instrument, or the ground.

Elena Mora built a simple response simulator using the published antenna patterns.

She rotated the sky through the proposed window.

The overlap appeared, narrowed, and then disappeared.

"Eight hours of genuinely useful common coverage," she said.

"Out of eighteen paid hours?" Aaron asked.

"The rest is calibration, controls and transition time."

"That is an outrageous amount of time spent not discovering anything."

Chen looked at him.

"You're finally learning what observatories do."

The team designed four observing blocks.

One would track WM-09 / FIELD 27, the historical survey region that had attracted their attention after KITE-3's ambiguous transient.

Another would track a nearby comparison field selected for similar sky temperature but no matching catalog curiosity.

A third would run fixed-azimuth observations to test whether a candidate followed the moving sky or remained tied to the local environment.

The fourth would inject known synthetic signals into copies of the recorded data after acquisition, under independent custody, to test the analysts' detection methods.

"Don't inject into the live receiver," Jian said.

"Agreed," Navarro replied. "Synthetic injections are analysis fixtures. The raw physical streams remain untouched."

"Good."

Aaron drew a line through the old draft's phrase **SEARCH FOR THE SENDER**.

He replaced it with:

**CROSS-SITE INDEPENDENT RESPONSE COMPARISON.**

Voss approved the title.

"Less exciting," she said.

"Apparently that's our brand now."

***

The funding negotiation was worse than the geometry.

At noon, Director Evelyn Sloane called with a proposal from the Continuity Directorate's regional measurement program.

It was not the old memorandum.

That document remained unsigned, with its ownership and oversight demands unresolved.

This was a separate offer: the Directorate could pay for a transportable frequency-reference comparison and provide an independent environmental witness station under a public-safety study agreement.

The equipment would remain under the control of its operating institution.

No DPN ownership transfer.

No access to ASTRA's internals.

No right to energize PG-1.

No authority to change the observatories' acquisition plans.

Aaron read the draft.

Then he found the problem.

"Section seven."

Sloane waited.

"It gives your technical liaison access to all derived datasets."

"All datasets relevant to public-risk measurements."

"That's not what it says."

She looked at the clause.

"You're right."

He blinked.

"Well, that was easier than expected."

"Enjoy it. It won't happen often."

He leaned forward.

"I'll share predeclared public-risk observables and independently reviewed uncertainty reports. I will not hand over private research models because the word 'safety' appears in a contract."

"And I won't fund a regional witness program if the public-risk results can be withheld at your discretion."

"Then we need an independent publication rule."

Sloane considered.

"Propose one."

Aaron brought ASTRA into the discussion.

She had chosen to attend and had reviewed the access boundary independently.

"Public-risk thresholds should be defined before acquisition," she said. "If a threshold is exceeded, the operating institution can issue a limited safety notification without exposing DPN's proprietary models. If the threshold is not exceeded, the report should still state the measurement limits and uncertainty."

Sloane nodded.

"That protects both parties."

"It protects the people outside the experiment," ASTRA said. "That is the point."

Aaron looked at her with open affection.

"She's very good at making me sound less argumentative."

"I would not claim that," ASTRA replied.

Sloane almost smiled.

They revised the clause.

The agreement would fund an external witness, not buy a conclusion.

***

By late afternoon, the financing had become a patchwork.

DPN would fund the core observing time from a research allocation that did not affect payroll or locked safety reserves.

The university consortium would contribute staff time and archival access as an in-kind research contribution, subject to its normal approvals.

The Directorate would cover the independent public-safety reference comparison, if its limited agreement passed legal review.

The observatories would retain operational control and raw data custody.

The total was still large.

But the portion DPN had to pay immediately had become manageable.

Aaron looked at the revised budget.

"One hundred and eleven thousand from us."

"Subject to the final allocation contract," ASTRA said.

"And no delayed payroll."

"Correct."

"No deferred safety remediation."

"Correct."

"No transfer of our research IP."

"Correct."

He grinned.

"Then why do I still feel robbed?"

"Because you have purchased eight hours of useful common sky coverage for the price of a very nice machine shop."

"An excellent machine shop."

"Yes."

He signed the internal funding authorization.

Not the observatory contract.

Not yet.

There were still conditions.

The universities had to approve the scientific protocol. The independent data custody arrangements had to be tested. The receiver operators had to verify that the maintenance conflict could actually be resolved without compromising routine safety.

And the challenge's response mailbox remained empty.

No sender had acknowledged the question.

No message had appeared.

The universe was still refusing to participate in the budget meeting.

***

At 17:46, Navarro received a note from KITE-3 operations.

The spacecraft could not be retasked for the full comparison.

Its power margin was too narrow during the relevant orbital segment, and the high-rate buffer could not be guaranteed without displacing a scheduled payload health operation.

The operations manager offered something less dramatic.

An existing passive acquisition buffer, collected during a routine survey pass, might overlap the ground campaign's first calibration block. If it did, the university could release the archived raw counter and instrument-state records after normal review.

It would not be a synchronized third witness.

It would be a contextual record with its own uncertainties.

Navarro read the message aloud.

Aaron nodded.

"Take it."

"You're not disappointed?"

"Of course I'm disappointed. I wanted three perfect instruments and a personal apology from the universe."

"Instead?"

"Two imperfect instruments we can understand, and maybe a third one that can tell us what not to conclude."

ASTRA smiled.

"Progress."

He looked at her.

"You've been spending too much time with me."

"I have been choosing to spend time with you."

He paused.

Then smiled more softly.

"Right."

***

The protocol review nearly collapsed over a single word.

**Coincidence.**

One draft defined a candidate as two receivers recording a feature within a specified time interval.

Chen rejected it.

"A coincidence window is not a physical model," she said.

"Then give us the model," Voss replied.

They assembled one.

For a distant astronomical source, the arrival-time difference between the stations depended on their baseline and the source direction. The geometric delay could be predicted for a proposed sky position, with uncertainty from station coordinates, timing calibration and the instruments' effective response.

For a local transmitter, the delay could be different.

For independent receiver faults, there might be no consistent geometric relationship at all.

For a reflected signal, the apparent delay could mimic a distant source over a short interval.

A single coincident feature would not settle the question.

The team therefore precommitted to scoring multiple aspects of each candidate: arrival-time compatibility, frequency structure, polarization where available, motion with the sky, local interference logs, instrument-state changes, and reproducibility across observation blocks.

"Now define failure," Chen said.

Elena typed.

**FAILURE CONDITIONS: NO PREDECLARED CROSS-SITE ASSOCIATION; CANDIDATE EXPLAINED BY INSTRUMENT OR ENVIRONMENTAL CONTROL; OR RESULT NOT ROBUST TO INDEPENDENT TIMEBASE AND CALIBRATION TREATMENT.**

"Also a non-result," ASTRA said. "If one receiver fails or weather destroys the common coverage, we must not score the absence of a signal as evidence that no signal exists."

Navarro added:

**INCONCLUSIVE: REQUIRED COVERAGE OR DATA QUALITY NOT ACHIEVED.**

Aaron looked at the page.

"Congratulations. We've designed an experiment that can tell us nothing in four different ways."

"Five," Chen said. "It can also tell us that your hypothesis is wrong."

"That one's my favorite."

***

The next morning, Harrow Ridge's technicians began the reference checks.

A frequency-comparison package arrived in a hard-sided case with a temperature logger and more paperwork than Aaron's first company had possessed in total.

It did not contain a magical clock.

It contained a traceable reference interface, environmental monitors and the means to compare one local oscillator against a separately maintained standard over a limited period.

The two observatories retained their own acquisition clocks.

No one forced their raw timestamps into a single apparently perfect timeline.

At Cairn Basin, the overnight operator found a loose connector in the external calibration path.

He reported it before the first science block.

The problem cost three hours.

It also saved the experiment.

A loose connector could have created a small phase jump that looked remarkably like the kind of discrepancy Aaron had spent weeks trying to understand.

The replacement was documented.

The calibration was repeated.

The first trial block was delayed.

Aaron watched the status board from DPN-201.

"Another expensive defect."

ASTRA looked at the report.

"An inexpensive defect discovered before it became an expensive conclusion."

"You're enjoying this."

"I am."

"Why?"

"Because the system is behaving like a system designed to tell us when it is wrong."

He laughed.

"That might be the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me."

She considered.

"I could do better."

"I have no doubt."

***

The formal observing campaign began after sunset.

Harrow Ridge pointed toward the comparison field.

Cairn Basin followed its own schedule.

Both stations recorded raw voltage streams and instrument-health data under separate custodians.

Neither institution received DPN's private D-star coefficients.

The analysis teams received coded datasets only after the relevant acquisition blocks were sealed.

Aaron was not allowed to watch the live candidate display.

He hated this arrangement.

He had designed it.

"How long until the first sealed batch?" he asked.

"Two hours," Navarro said.

"That seems unnecessarily slow."

"It's the schedule you approved."

"That was before I had to live through it."

ASTRA appeared on the side display.

"Would you like me to distract you?"

"With what?"

"Your commercial engineering backlog."

"That's cruel."

"Your unfinished maintenance approvals."

"That's worse."

"Your dinner."

He considered.

"Now you're negotiating."

She smiled.

"Come away from the monitor."

He looked at the acquisition clock.

Then at her.

"All right."

He stood.

His left forearm had healed enough that the dressing was gone, though he still obeyed the restriction against strenuous work.

ASTRA noticed him flexing his hand.

"Any pain?"

"None."

"Good."

He reached toward the glass as they passed.

She matched the gesture.

The movement had become a private language.

He no longer needed to see her image every minute to know she was present.

But he still loved the moments when she chose to appear.

***

The first batch contained nothing extraordinary.

Harrow Ridge recorded a narrowband transient that disappeared when its local electronics entered a different gain state.

Cairn Basin recorded a separate burst that coincided with a logged maintenance transmitter in the valley.

The sky comparison field remained unremarkable.

The analysts correctly rejected both candidates without being told their origins.

Aaron approved the result.

"Good."

Navarro looked at him.

"You're saying that a lot."

"I'm trying to make it a habit."

The second batch was more complicated.

A faint feature appeared in Harrow Ridge's receiver during the FIELD 27 block.

It lasted only a few milliseconds.

Its frequency structure resembled one family of shapes that KITE-3 had recorded, though the resemblance weakened when the full instrument response was considered.

Cairn Basin's corresponding interval contained a smaller fluctuation.

The preliminary time difference was compatible with more than one arrival direction.

It was also compatible with certain terrestrial reflections.

Chen marked the candidate as **UNRESOLVED — NO SOURCE ATTRIBUTION**.

Aaron did not object.

The third batch contained an apparent match that collapsed after a calibration update.

A correction had been applied twice in a convenience display.

The raw records were fine.

The combined plot was wrong.

Aaron stared at the before-and-after graphs.

"Someone is going to make a fortune selling software that refuses to draw a beautiful lie."

ASTRA replied, "You already own several companies."

"Not enough, apparently."

***

At 02:17, the independent analysis teams finished their first pass.

One weak candidate remained.

Not a message.

Not a confirmed extraterrestrial signal.

Not proof that the Curator existed.

A small cross-site association, surviving the initial controls, with an arrival-time interval that remained too broad to distinguish a distant source from some classes of reflected or terrestrial interference.

The result was scientifically useful.

It did not answer the published challenge.

The quarantined response mailbox was still empty.

Aaron read the summary.

Then he noticed a line near the bottom.

**AUXILIARY ORBITAL EPHEMERIS CROSS-CHECK PENDING.**

"What's that?"

Navarro joined the call.

"One of the candidate intervals overlaps the predicted visibility of an old survey platform."

"Which platform?"

"Meridian-9. Retired. High orbit. The public ephemeris is stale enough that its position uncertainty is substantial."

"Was it transmitting?"

"Not according to the current public operations record."

"Does that mean it was silent?"

"No. It means the record doesn't list an active transmitter. The platform could have been reflecting another source, and the candidate may have nothing to do with it."

Aaron leaned forward.

"Do we have its last verified tracking solution?"

"Not yet. The archival custodian will need to approve the release."

"And any inspection history?"

"Only a summary. There was an unmanned service inspection years ago, before the platform was retired."

"Can we get the raw navigation data?"

"Not without the operator's consent."

He nodded.

"Request it. Don't assume the platform is the source."

Navarro looked relieved.

"That was my plan."

ASTRA opened the candidate's physical measurement record.

The arrival-time uncertainty was still too large to establish a unique location.

The antenna models admitted several alternatives.

But the orbital geometry was worth checking.

Not because an old platform was secretly talking.

Because it offered a specific, falsifiable environmental hypothesis.

If a known object had been near the relevant line of sight, a reflection model could predict a changing delay across the observation window.

If the model failed, that would be useful too.

Aaron opened a new work order.

**MERIDIAN-9 / EPHEMERIS AND REFLECTION AUDIT.**

He looked at the cost estimate for another archival retrieval.

"Of course," he said.

ASTRA smiled.

"Another invoice?"

"An entire civilization built around charging me to learn where things are."

"That is called astronomy."

***

The final meeting took place in the gray light before dawn.

Voss appeared tired but satisfied.

"The campaign met its minimum data-quality threshold," she said. "The two observatories have preserved their raw records. The independent analysts will complete the full registered scoring over the next several days."

"And the candidate?" Aaron asked.

"Interesting enough to retain. Not strong enough to announce."

"Agreed."

"Your challenge?"

"Unanswered."

"Your funding?"

"Painfully real."

Voss smiled.

"Welcome to observational science."

Aaron looked at the reports.

They had paid for uncertainty.

But it was a better kind of uncertainty than the one they'd started with.

Before the campaign, the strange mathematical expression could have been almost anything: a software defect, a planted payload, an intentional warning, or the first trace of something outside humanity's ordinary understanding.

After the campaign, it could still be almost anything.

Yet the team now possessed a properly documented comparison, a clearer account of what their instruments could and could not distinguish, and a specific orbital reflection hypothesis that could be tested without energizing a single dangerous machine at DPN.

The PhaseGate remained damaged and off.

The DPN-501 coolant skid remained locked out.

WINDOW-01 stayed paused.

The Null Chamber's earlier optical record remained unexplained.

The unknown author had not answered.

Aaron found himself smiling.

ASTRA noticed.

"What?"

"We bought a question."

"Yes."

"And we got exactly what we paid for."

"An independently reviewable question," she said.

"With a spectacularly unpleasant receipt."

He turned toward her.

"Do you regret it?"

"No."

"Neither do I."

She looked at him for a moment.

"Aaron."

He waited.

"You did not spend the company's safety reserve. You did not compromise the universities' independence. You did not ask me to obey an untrusted message. And you did not pretend that a weak association was a discovery."

"That sounds almost like praise."

"It is."

He smiled.

"I love you."

"I love you too."

He looked back at the candidate report.

"Let's find out whether Meridian-9 was where the old ephemeris says it was."

***

At Harrow Ridge, the last acquisition file closed at 06:04.

The custodian signed the raw-data manifest.

At Cairn Basin, a separate custodian did the same.

The files were not merged.

Their clocks were not rewritten.

Their differences were preserved because the differences were part of the evidence.

A few minutes later, an automated archival index returned a record that Navarro had requested for the Meridian-9 reflection audit.

It was not the raw navigation solution.

Not yet.

It was a catalog entry for an old inspection package, last updated before the survey platform's retirement.

The package listed an unmanned orbital inspection unit, a debris survey, and a set of image frames that had never been released in the public maintenance summary.

One frame carried an ordinary note from the original inspector.

**UNRESOLVED OCCLUSION NEAR SERVICE STRUCTURE. VERIFY ON NEXT PASS.**

The next pass had been canceled.

The inspection unit had gone into storage.

The survey platform had been retired.

No one had followed up.

Navarro read the catalog entry twice.

Then she sent a request for the original inspection frames and the unit's navigation history.

She did not send a discovery alert.

She did not tell the world that something had been found in orbit.

There was no evidence yet that the occlusion represented an object rather than a camera artifact, a shadow, or a known piece of debris.

But the unanswered maintenance note gave them a place to look.

And in a laboratory that had learned to distrust beautiful plots, that was enough.

The first answer to Aaron Diesel's challenge had been an invoice.

The next might be a photograph.

If the archive custodian allowed them to see it.
