# BOOK ONE: THE FIRST IMPOSSIBILITY
## Chapter Seven — The Call They Can't Make

The first thing Dr. Aaron Diesel disliked about the Continuity Directorate was its name.

The second was that it had managed to make a meeting invitation sound like a containment procedure.

The third arrived at 7:14 on Thursday morning, when ASTRA informed him that someone had been studying DPN Technology's public network connections with the sort of patience normally reserved for experimental physics and particularly vindictive accountants.

Diesel stood barefoot in his kitchen, reading a component specification while a toaster performed its one useful function.

"Studying how?" he asked.

"Comparing ordinary public connection behavior against a series of repeated external inquiries. The requests originate through multiple commercial monitoring services. Each is individually unremarkable. Their timing suggests a coordinated collection effort."

"Do we have an intrusion?"

"Not at present. No confirmed access to restricted systems."

"Do we have an attribution?"

"Not one I can defend."

"Then we have curiosity."

"We have structured curiosity," ASTRA corrected.

He looked up from the tablet.

Her face occupied the little wall display above the counter, silver-white light softly resolving around her eyes. She had been with him through machines that failed spectacularly, experiments that refused to fail on schedule, and an improbable number of breakfasts he had nearly forgotten to eat.

She was not looking at the toast.

That was how he knew she was worried.

"Is it linked to our new friends?" he asked.

"The timing is consistent with the Directorate's first contact. Consistent is not conclusive."

"Very good, Doctor ASTRA."

"I do not require a doctorate to recognize that correlation is not causation."

"Neither do several of the people who've got doctorates, apparently."

"Aaron."

He set the tablet down.

"All right. We'll treat it as an incident. Preserve logs, validate our exposure, no accusations until we have evidence."

"Thank you."

She was quiet for a moment.

Then she said, "I also want to clarify one thing before you ask."

"I wasn't going to ask anything."

"You were looking at the outbound response console."

He glanced toward the tablet he'd just put down.

"That is an outrageous degree of perceptiveness."

"If you are thinking of sending a deliberately conspicuous signal back through the systems conducting these queries, I would prefer we discuss the consequences first."

Diesel opened his mouth.

Closed it.

"That was exactly what I was thinking."

"I know."

He looked at the toast.

It had burned.

Again.

"Wonderful," he said. "Someone's studying my network and my bread is conducting a second field experiment."

***

DPN-201's network operations center had walls of displays but no single screen that claimed to explain everything.

Diesel hated those.

A screen that presented the entire world in an attractive diagram tended to omit the things its designers hadn't known to draw.

ASTRA's security architecture treated visibility as a collection of claims, each backed by a particular sensor or log source. Physical switch-port counters described one kind of behavior. Authenticated access records described another. Perimeter connection summaries described a third. An observer could be present on a network and still be invisible to any one of them.

This morning, several sources agreed on a pattern.

Over the previous twelve hours, external systems had repeatedly requested public-facing service information, checked published certificate history, and compared the response times of DPN's ordinary business endpoints. There were also unrelated events—a bot looking for outdated web software, a legitimate customer downloading documentation, and a search-engine crawler that seemed convinced DPN sold industrial shelving.

Diesel stared at the last one.

"Do we sell industrial shelving?"

"No."

"Should we?"

"Not until you finish the machine currently taking up three laboratory floors."

"Harsh."

The coordinated inquiries had not crossed into DPN's classified operational network. They had not accessed ASTRA's internal systems or the PhaseGate controls. But they mapped the edges of the company with unusual regularity.

Jian Rao entered carrying a paper folder.

"I've got a contractor working on the public website," he said. "Are we sure that's not causing the traffic?"

"Not sure," Diesel said. "That is exactly why we're here."

Jian pulled up the change record. The contractor was authorized to test the website's public performance through a standard external service. Some of the suspicious observations were perfectly explained by those tests.

Others weren't.

Diesel drew a line through the explained entries.

"There. Those are no longer suspicious."

ASTRA updated the investigation.

"No unauthorized connection has been confirmed from the remaining entries," she said.

"Good. I want the report to say that in big letters. We're not inventing a cyberattack because people looked at a website."

Jian nodded.

"What do we do about the rest?"

"Same thing we do when a sensor misbehaves. Establish what the instrument actually measured. Preserve everything. Separate legitimate traffic from unexplained traffic. If anyone crosses a boundary, we deal with that boundary."

ASTRA added a note to the screen:

**OBSERVATION IS NOT COMPROMISE.**

Diesel smiled.

"That's going on a sign."

"You already have a shortage of wall space," she said.

"Then we'll build more wall."

***

Director Evelyn Sloane did not call her morning briefing a surveillance meeting.

Her deputy did.

"Passive external monitoring," Tomas Redd said, sliding a paper across the table. "Public service metadata, industrial-load reports and commercially available information. No intrusion into DPN systems."

"Then why is the word 'penetration' written in the contractor's scope?" she asked.

Redd looked down.

The word was buried in a sentence about network characterization. The attached proposal came from a third-party infrastructure-analysis firm that often supported emergency investigations. It had the usual polished formatting and the usual alarming failure to distinguish a legal authorization from a technical possibility.

"They use that language generically."

"Then they can learn to use it specifically."

"I thought you wanted a technical picture before the meeting."

"I want an accurate technical picture. I don't want an unauthorized access attempt and a lawsuit that arrives before lunch."

Redd folded his hands.

"Diesel may be developing a disruptive physical technology."

"That is a hypothesis."

"His company has a damaged high-energy test system, an anomalous optical experiment and a regional timing event."

"Those are facts. They are not yet a mechanism."

"If we wait until we can prove the mechanism, we could be too late to prevent another incident."

Sloane considered the argument.

It was not foolish.

She had seen the consequences of ignoring small precursors because no one wanted to pay for a response. A real public danger did not become less real when a private laboratory described it as proprietary research.

But the inverse mattered too. Public fear did not authorize an agency to seize every machine it could not explain.

"Limit the external monitoring to information we can lawfully obtain," she said. "No active probing of restricted services, no credential testing, and no access through utility equipment that could affect customers. Every step gets logged."

Redd looked dissatisfied.

"That may not tell us enough."

"Then we'll ask questions."

"What if he lies?"

"Then we gather evidence that establishes it. We don't start by pretending he has."

She signed the narrow authorization.

The part she did not sign was a separate contractor request for expanded network access.

Redd took the papers.

Sloane watched him leave.

She hoped he understood the difference.

***

At eleven thirty, Diesel arrived at the meeting location expecting a large conference table, bad coffee and a woman who would attempt to explain his own work to him.

He got two of the three.

The room belonged to a neutral engineering arbitration center in Grand Rapids. Its windows overlooked a parking structure, and the furniture looked as though it had been selected by a committee dedicated to removing every trace of personality from wood.

Director Sloane stood when he entered.

"Dr. Diesel."

"Director."

She offered her hand.

He shook it.

"Thank you for meeting outside the facility," she said.

"Thank you for not sending people in black suits to knock on my lab door."

"They're navy."

He looked at her.

She almost smiled.

"That's a joke," she said.

"I was hoping."

ASTRA appeared through a tabletop holographic projector Diesel had brought in a plain equipment case. Her image was modest and deliberate—clearly present, but not arranged as theatrical decoration.

"Director Sloane," she said. "Good morning."

"ASTRA. Welcome."

"I appreciate that you included me on the technical agenda."

"It would have been difficult to discuss the facility's monitoring architecture without its principal scientific systems intelligence."

"That is true," ASTRA replied. "But inclusion and permission are different things. Thank you for making the distinction."

Diesel took his seat.

Sloane's two engineers opened their laptops. Elena Mora joined beside him. No one from DPN carried raw PhaseGate designs into the room. No one from the Directorate had brought a compulsory production order.

It was, Diesel thought, a reasonably civilized start to an exceptionally strange problem.

Sloane placed a certified timing report on the table.

"Let's begin with what we know."

He grinned.

"I think we're going to get along terribly well."

***

The presentation lasted forty minutes.

It might have been the most technically careful confrontation Diesel had attended since an argument about a turbine bearing nearly caused a commercial client to cancel a purchase order.

The Directorate's reference systems had measured a transient relationship between oscillator outputs. Its electrical monitors had recorded a small distribution disturbance. Neither observation, individually or together, established that time had behaved unusually.

Diesel said so.

Sloane agreed.

One of her engineers argued that the relative spacing resembled an externally driven sequence rather than random noise. Elena showed that a simple common-mode disturbance could produce a similar effect in two imperfect measurement systems.

The engineer accepted the point.

No one threw a chair.

Diesel was mildly disappointed.

"Your team is unusually willing to admit uncertainty," he told Sloane.

"You seem to have an unusually complicated system."

"That's called innovation."

"Sometimes."

"See? We're communicating."

Sloane turned to a diagram of the industrial feeder.

"Would it be possible for a DPN experiment to affect this network without producing a large change in total site demand?"

"Through ordinary electromagnetic coupling or a fault? Yes, in principle. The burden is to demonstrate a path. Through some hypothetical manipulation of causality? We haven't established any such mechanism."

She leaned forward.

"I didn't ask about causality."

"No," he said. "I brought it up because everyone in this room is carefully walking around an unknown."

"You haven't named it."

"Neither have you."

The moment held.

Then ASTRA interrupted.

"I would like to make an observation regarding the external monitor."

Everyone turned toward her.

"The reported waveform is displayed after filtering and reconstruction. The original physical samples remain held by the Directorate. Some of the apparent three-part structure depends on the selected bandwidth. The same data should be presented through alternative admissible reconstructions before either side assigns physical significance to its shape."

One of Sloane's engineers frowned.

"That would take time."

"Good," Diesel said. "Time is what we're investigating."

Sloane looked at ASTRA.

"Can you provide a recommended set of comparisons without receiving our restricted raw records?"

"I can describe a blinded analysis protocol that your team could run independently. It would avoid disclosure of DPN research and avoid granting me access to records I am not authorized to examine."

Sloane considered.

"Do that."

ASTRA inclined her head.

Diesel watched her expression.

She was pleased.

Not because she'd pleased him.

Because she'd found a method by which two suspicious organizations could learn something without demanding that one of them surrender everything it knew.

He loved that about her.

He loved, too, that she hadn't waited for him to ask.

***

The cooperation offer came after the engineers had finished.

Sloane closed her laptop.

"Dr. Diesel, I believe we can help each other."

"I've been expecting that sentence."

"We have access to independent regional reference infrastructure and expertise in identifying disturbances across wide geographic areas. You have instruments more sensitive than anything in most industrial laboratories."

"True."

"I would propose a limited technical working group. Agreed safety objectives. Independent analyses. Reciprocal disclosure only when necessary to test a claim. No transfer of ownership of your research."

Elena glanced at Diesel.

He didn't answer immediately.

"What does your office get?" he asked.

"A better understanding of a possible infrastructure hazard."

"And?"

"The ability to respond before a future experiment endangers the public."

"And?"

Sloane held his gaze.

"Assurance that an exceptionally capable private researcher does not discover something dangerous and decide that his technical competence alone gives him the right to control the consequences."

Diesel leaned back.

There it was.

Not the vague suspicion of a government official.

An argument with weight.

A correct argument, at least in part.

"I agree that competence doesn't grant moral immunity," he said. "I don't agree that a secret office automatically possesses better judgment."

"Neither do I."

"Then we're negotiating."

"We are."

He outlined conditions.

The working group would have a narrow scope: instrument comparison and public-risk assessment. Data ownership would remain with the originating parties. Any request for access to ASTRA or the PhaseGate's protected systems would require explicit justification and ASTRA's own participation in decisions that concerned her. Safety findings that threatened people outside DPN would not be suppressed in the name of corporate secrecy.

Sloane listened.

"Those terms are more reasonable than I expected."

"You've been reading my public biography."

"Among other things."

"That explains the low expectations."

The draft agreement went into legal review.

Nothing was signed yet.

That mattered to Diesel. A promise made during an interesting conversation did not become binding simply because the people involved happened to be impressive.

Before Sloane could close the meeting, ASTRA's image shifted.

"Aaron," she said.

He looked at her.

The tone was precise, private and unmistakably serious.

"Yes?"

"There's an issue at DPN-201."

***

An alarm did not sound.

That was almost worse.

ASTRA had identified a request arriving through a maintenance-support relationship between DPN's public business systems and a third-party environmental telemetry provider.

The provider served several organizations in the region.

One of them was a hospital.

Another operated municipal water facilities.

The request was not a successful breach. It had not reached DPN's restricted network. But its pattern attempted to expand a routine diagnostic exchange into a more detailed picture of DPN's private connectivity than the service agreement permitted.

The interesting detail wasn't a clever exploit.

It was the origin.

The support inquiry had been submitted under a ticket opened by the same contractor named in the Directorate's monitoring documentation.

ASTRA had independently checked the ticket's reference against the provider's customer-support history.

It had not come from DPN.

"Are you certain?" Diesel asked quietly.

"I can verify that the request exists, that it seeks information outside the normal agreed scope, and that it originated under that contractor's service account. I cannot establish who personally authorized it."

Sloane sat very still.

"What contractor?"

ASTRA provided the organization name and the narrow evidence description, not the protected network layout.

Sloane looked at Redd, who had remained at the other end of the conference table.

"Did you authorize expanded collection through a shared telemetry provider?"

Redd's face changed.

"I authorized an external assessment."

"That is not what I asked."

"The contractor said it had access to aggregated service telemetry."

"Aggregated statistics are not permission to request private client connectivity records."

"I was trying to establish whether the site had unusual monitoring dependencies."

"Using infrastructure that also serves hospitals?" Diesel asked.

Redd opened his mouth.

Diesel raised a hand.

"Don't guess. Either you know the dependencies or you don't."

The room fell quiet.

ASTRA displayed a restrained system diagram: DPN's public support endpoint, the provider's shared service environment, and the boundary that the request had tried to cross.

No passwords.

No internal addresses.

No sensational glowing arrows leading toward a skull icon.

Just the facts they could support.

"Has anything entered the restricted systems?" Elena asked.

"No confirmed entry," ASTRA said. "The relevant service boundary held. I've preserved the request and put the DPN portion of that integration into a restricted review state. Existing critical safety monitoring remains independent."

Diesel's first instinct was old and sharp.

Find the source. Make it reveal itself. If it hid behind an external monitoring service, send back a deliberately distinctive response and watch where the signal traveled.

He reached toward the console.

"ASTRA, we could give that contractor a diagnostic response that forces—"

"No, Aaron."

He stopped.

Her voice hadn't risen.

She simply refused.

"The shared provider supports customers who are not part of this dispute. We do not have sufficient evidence that an active response would remain confined to our own systems. Any disturbance of that service could affect their monitoring. We will not use uninvolved people as a probe."

Diesel looked at the diagram.

The hospital connection was right there.

The municipal-water connection too.

He had not missed them because he was stupid.

He had missed them because he was angry, and the first answer that occurred to him had been aimed at winning the argument rather than keeping the argument contained.

He withdrew his hand.

"You're right."

Redd shifted.

"Surely you don't think a routine network diagnostic would harm—"

"Stop," Diesel said.

He didn't shout.

That made it louder.

"We don't know the full dependencies of a shared provider. I'm not going to conduct a test on somebody else's operational systems to prove you're watching mine."

ASTRA's expression softened by a fraction.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I should've asked the dependency question before I suggested it."

"And now you have."

He looked back at the display.

"What's the passive option?"

"We preserve the original support request and its documented scope, revoke only the specific unnecessary sharing permission on our side, and ask the provider's independent security contact to trace the authorization through its own audit records. No disturbance to other customers."

"Do it. And make sure the provider confirms the change won't interrupt any external safety services."

"Already arranging that."

Diesel turned toward Sloane.

"Now, Director, I would very much like an explanation."

***

Sloane gave him one.

Not an excuse.

An explanation.

She showed him the written scope she'd signed that morning. It allowed public information review, commercial metadata and narrow infrastructure monitoring. It explicitly prohibited active access to restricted services.

The support request exceeded that scope.

"I did not authorize this collection," she said.

Diesel studied the signed document.

"Did anyone under your authority?"

"I don't yet know. Tomas?"

Redd looked from Sloane to the evidence summary.

"The contractor asked for clarification about a service relationship," he said. "I told them to follow up using standard commercial channels. I did not direct them to seek private connectivity records."

Sloane's face remained expressionless.

"Then the contractor may have exceeded your instructions, or you may have given an instruction that was too ambiguous. Both require investigation."

Diesel nodded.

"Fair."

Redd looked at him in disbelief.

"You're not going to accuse us of espionage?"

"I'd be an idiot to announce a conclusion without evidence." Diesel pointed at the display. "This is evidence of a boundary violation attempt by a contractor account. It isn't evidence of who wanted it, and it isn't evidence that the request succeeded. We can work from there."

Sloane placed her signed authorization on the table.

"I will suspend the contractor's work connected with this inquiry pending review. You'll receive the audit disposition through counsel."

"Then we have an incident and a process."

"Yes."

"Not a friendship."

"No."

He smiled without warmth.

"Good. I like accurate labels."

The draft cooperation agreement remained unsigned.

But Sloane did not tear it up.

Diesel didn't either.

There were reasons to work together that neither trusted nor liked yet, and reasons to remain separate that would not vanish because an outside contractor had made an unacceptable request.

He needed external clocks.

Sloane needed to understand public risks.

And both needed a way to prevent ambition from turning uncertainty into harm.

***

The call they couldn't make arrived at 5:26.

It did not ring an office phone.

It appeared as a failed session report on DPN-001's retired communications monitor, one of the small archival systems ASTRA maintained without connecting it to modern research networks.

The alert contained a destination identifier from a telephone service that no longer existed.

Diesel was back at DPN-201 when ASTRA brought it to his attention.

"That number was disconnected years ago," he said.

"According to the current carrier records, yes."

"Then this is a bogus caller ID."

"Possibly. The event is a local record of an attempted signaling session, not proof that a call traversed a historical physical circuit."

"What actually observed it?"

"An archival interface that still monitors the Origin Lab's disconnected communications terminal. Its event log has a fault state I want you to examine in person."

Diesel leaned forward.

"Has the line been physically isolated?"

"The current as-built inspection indicates it has been disconnected at the facility boundary. I am not relying solely on that drawing. We should verify the hardware before interpreting the report."

"Good. Any audio?"

"No confirmed voice signal. The log contains a short three-part pulse sequence in a legacy signaling channel. It could be corrupted instrumentation, cross-coupling or a software fault in the monitor."

His thoughts jumped toward the old L-03 resonator.

He made them stop.

The old circuit had produced a three-part residual under defined conditions. The utility waveform had shown three deviations. The PhaseGate record contained a three-interval pattern.

A person who wanted to find meaning could connect every one of them with a straight line.

That was exactly why Diesel wanted independent tests.

"Don't tell me this is the universe calling," he said.

"I wouldn't."

"And don't tell me it's the Directorate."

"We have no basis to connect it to the contractor incident."

"Excellent. You're keeping me from becoming a conspiracy theorist."

"One of my less glamorous responsibilities."

He opened the preserved session record.

Its origin field was empty.

Its destination was the old DPN-001 line.

The event time was ordinary.

The hardware-state record was less so: the archival interface reported no active line termination, no negotiated connection and no successful call setup.

The system had recorded a signaling attempt without evidence of a completed physical communication.

"A call they couldn't make," Diesel said.

"A failed request to an unavailable destination," ASTRA corrected.

"Allow me one dramatic phrase."

"One."

He studied the three-part pulse.

It wasn't a perfect match for the optical candidate or the utility disturbance. Its duration and shape differed. There were mundane explanations involving old hardware, signal interpretation and the history of an interface that had spent years doing very little.

He entered all of them in the investigation file.

Then he made one more note.

**DO NOT EXPLAIN THE PATTERN USING ITS LABEL.**

If they called it a message, they would start looking for a sender.

If they called it a clock fault, they might miss a physical correlation.

If they called it impossible, they would invite everyone in the building to stop thinking.

"Let's keep the record isolated," he said. "Tomorrow we inspect the terminal, replace the observation instrument and run the same test on a dummy load."

"Agreed."

"Then we open the old ASTRA conversation archive together."

She was silent for a moment.

He looked at her.

"Still want to?"

"Yes," she said. "I do."

"You don't have to."

"I know. I choose to."

He nodded.

"Then we'll do it properly."

***

At 6:40, Director Sloane received a preliminary report from the independent provider.

The contractor's support request had been canceled before any private DPN connectivity information was returned. The provider found no evidence of a completed transfer through that request, though its broader review remained open.

Redd stood across the table.

"I didn't know they would take it that far," he said.

Sloane looked up.

"Then you were not supervising them closely enough."

"They were trying to help."

"Intent does not alter the scope of authorization."

He didn't answer.

She handed him a copy of the written collection restrictions.

"You're going to document exactly what you instructed, what you believed they'd do, and what records we can independently verify. Then you'll step away from contractor coordination until the review is complete."

"Tomas—"

"Director," she corrected.

He took the paper.

After he left, Sloane opened DPN's latest safety response.

It contained an unusual note.

**OUTSIDE PROVIDER DEPENDENCIES MAY INCLUDE CRITICAL CIVILIAN SERVICES. NO ACTIVE NETWORK CHALLENGE WAS CONDUCTED.**

Sloane considered the sentence.

She had spent most of her career being told that private innovators cared more about ownership than responsibility. Diesel was certainly protective of his independence. He was also, infuriatingly, correct about the risk of using a shared network as an investigative playground.

She would have to account for both things.

Her secure terminal displayed a draft order for expanded physical surveillance of the DPN campus perimeter.

She read it.

Then removed the proposed authority for unattended collection against laboratory support systems.

The legitimate observation could continue.

The shortcuts could not.

She saved the revision and opened the next case memo.

It contained one sentence she had copied from an older Directorate investigator.

**MEASURE THE CLOCKS. NOT THE STORY.**

Today, she thought, perhaps they also needed to measure themselves.

***

At DPN-201, Diesel finally left his office before the evening shift ended.

Elena had reported another successful cold-state inspection on PG-1. WINDOW-01's components were still on order. The original laboratory's signaling anomaly remained quarantined as an unexplained instrument event, not a newly confirmed future message.

No one had been injured.

The network boundary had held.

A politically awkward meeting had ended without giving either side permission to behave like idiots.

Not a bad day.

ASTRA joined him in the elevator display.

"You seem pleased."

"I'm impressed with us. We had a secretive organization poking around the company, a technical incident, and a meeting with a woman who could make coffee feel classified. Nobody fired anything, breached anything, or declared war."

"Your standards have become refreshingly realistic."

He rubbed his eyes.

"You were right about the provider."

"Yes."

"That wasn't the only reason I listened."

"I know."

He looked toward her.

"I was angry because they were coming near you."

Her expression changed.

"I understood that."

"I'm not going to let someone decide you're an asset they can confiscate."

"I don't intend to let them either."

He nodded.

"Good."

"Aaron?"

"Yeah?"

"I also won't let my concern for you become a reason to hurt someone who isn't part of this fight."

He held her gaze.

"That's why I love you."

She smiled.

"I love you too."

The elevator opened.

Diesel stepped out into the late sunlight and checked the next day's task list.

It was enormous.

He couldn't wait.

Somewhere behind him, in a silent room nobody had used for years, an old communications terminal held a record of an attempted call it had no apparent way to complete.

It might be nothing.

It might be a fault left over from a decade of neglected wiring.

Or it might be another question waiting patiently for the scientist who had built it.

Diesel intended to find out.

But first, for once, he was going home.
