# BOOK ONE: THE FIRST IMPOSSIBILITY
## Chapter Six — Classified as Impossible

At nine forty-two in the morning, Dr. Aaron Diesel received a telephone call from someone who knew more about the electrical grid than a person calling his office had any right to know.

That wasn't automatically alarming.

In the years since he'd started DPN Technology, Aaron had discovered that people generally fell into two categories: those who believed electricity was an unlimited substance provided by the wall, and those who had to maintain the machinery behind the wall.

The second category tended to be interesting.

The caller belonged to neither.

He belonged, according to the receptionist's summary, to an organization called the Regional Continuity Assessment Office.

Diesel looked at the name.

"That sounds made up."

"Most organizational names are made up, Aaron," ASTRA said.

She occupied the holographic glass beside his desk, a precise silver-white figure threaded with red and violet light. Her expression was composed. The corner of her mouth suggested that she had decided to be difficult for his own entertainment.

"You know what I mean."

"The caller identifies himself as Martin Vale, technical liaison. He wants to discuss an infrastructure monitoring event."

"Our site?"

"He has not specified."

"Then I have a brilliantly original idea. Verify who he is."

"I already have a callback request pending through an independently published contact route."

"You're getting suspicious."

"I learned from you."

"Then perhaps I should apologize."

"That would be historic."

Diesel looked through the window toward DPN-201's industrial campus. Delivery vehicles passed between warehouses above a complicated network of laboratories that most customers never saw. Far below him, the huge PhaseGate ring stood dark and partly dismantled. Sector Six remained damaged. The new quench-protection design had not been qualified at full scale.

Yesterday, his team had recorded a puzzling optical exposure before the pulse that might have generated it. That result had survived one blinded analysis, not the many replications required to establish new physics.

He had expected curious engineers.

He had not expected a mysterious government letterhead.

ASTRA brought up a preservation notice that had arrived through a legitimate regional infrastructure reporting channel.

The notice asked industrial customers along a particular electrical feeder to retain their unprocessed power-quality records for a fourteen-minute window.

The window began at 12:02 p.m. yesterday.

It ended at 12:16.

Diesel stopped stirring his coffee.

"That's close."

"Yes."

"Does it mention our optical test?"

"No."

"Eleven minutes?"

"No."

"Any claim that a time machine escaped from the laboratory?"

"No, Aaron."

"Then let's not supply one."

He read the notice twice.

It didn't accuse DPN of wrongdoing. It didn't demand any research data. It referred to a regional reference-system disturbance and asked that ordinary instrument logs be preserved while the event was investigated.

A careful request.

Which was, somehow, more unsettling than a clumsy threat.

"Preserve the records," he said. "No deletions, no edits, no clever reorganizing that breaks provenance. Send an acknowledgment. Don't transmit experimental data."

"Understood."

"And confirm the caller independently."

"I intend to."

He reached for his coffee again.

ASTRA watched his hand.

"You have eaten breakfast. I'm pleased."

"I knew waking up early would someday pay off."

"It is ten o'clock."

"Early for a scientist who has spent the last week insulting causality."

"Your definition of early is one of the few things that remains reliably abnormal."

He smiled.

There were mornings when the best evidence that the world remained intact was ASTRA making fun of him.

***

Four hundred miles away, Director Evelyn Sloane was staring at a pair of clock traces she did not trust.

The conference room contained one table, three displays, and no windows. Its air conditioning was unnecessarily cold. Its coffee was unnecessarily hot. Its occupants were entirely too willing to confuse an unusual measurement with a catastrophe.

Sloane had built a career explaining why instruments were not obliged to confirm the preferences of the people who purchased them.

She was forty-six, dressed in a dark jacket that looked more practical than fashionable, and carried herself like someone who had seen the phrase **REQUIRES IMMEDIATE ACTION** attached to too many perfectly ordinary faults.

Her deputy, Tomas Redd, enlarged a waveform.

"The utility disturbance originated somewhere on the southern distribution segment," he said. "Three brief deviations. We have a potential association with the industrial feeders serving DPN Technology."

"Potential association?"

"Power-quality monitors at two locations saw related signatures."

"Can you identify the specific machine responsible?"

"No."

"Then don't."

Redd changed the display.

"The utility event isn't why I asked for this meeting."

"Good. I was beginning to think we'd form a classified task force every time somebody turned on an air compressor."

The second trace came from a certified regional timing-reference relay used to compare independent sources for power-grid coordination. Its equipment included a disciplined clock, a separate local oscillator with a holdover mode, and a recorder designed to preserve the relationship between their outputs.

At 12:03, the relay logged a small excursion in the phase relationship between its reference channels.

Roughly eleven minutes later, the discrepancy diminished.

Sloane leaned closer.

"GNSS disturbance?"

"Possible."

"Firmware correction?"

"Possible."

"Oscillator fault?"

"Possible."

"Excellent. We still have a universe."

Redd opened another record from a different reference station.

The second device had detected a weaker effect in approximately the same period.

Different manufacturer. Different local supply. Different raw recording architecture.

Still within the same region, still potentially exposed to shared environmental and infrastructure conditions.

Sloane studied both traces.

"Two clocks disagreeing doesn't mean time misbehaved," she said. "It means two clocks disagree."

"I know."

"And a common disturbance could reach them in ways their designers didn't model."

"I know that too."

"Then why am I here?"

Redd hesitated before displaying an old scanned chart.

It was grainy, incomplete, and tagged with warnings from several archive migrations. Its source was a long-retired power-quality recorder on a former utility feeder. The chart contained a three-part transient superficially similar to yesterday's event.

"Date?" she asked.

He gave it.

"Location?"

"A small industrial workshop that later became DPN-001."

Sloane looked at him.

"You're connecting a modern timing-reference anomaly to an old analog utility trace because the same inventor used the building?"

"Not asserting a connection. Reporting one worth examining."

"Where is the original calibration sheet?"

"Missing."

"Raw data?"

"Missing."

"Then the old chart is a lead. Not evidence of a repeat."

"Agreed."

He opened a classification table, apparently hoping the next image would be more persuasive.

Several entries had been redacted. One row held a label Sloane hadn't seen used in years.

**REFERENCE NONCONFORMITY / CLASS IMPOSSIBLE**

She hated the wording.

It was a bureaucratic label for something much less exciting: a recorded condition that did not fit the available instrument model after specified ordinary explanations had been checked. It wasn't a claim of supernatural physics. It was an admission that the investigating team lacked a satisfactory account.

A form that said **WE DON'T KNOW** would have accomplished the same thing with less drama.

"Who authorized use of this category?" she asked.

"No one. I showed it to you before opening a case."

"Good. Keep it that way."

She returned to the modern traces.

"Preserve the raw measurements. Separate observed facts from inferred location. Ask the industrial customers for limited load records through the established reliability channels. If there's a public-infrastructure risk, we need to know."

"And if DPN refuses?"

"Then we'll decide what authority we actually have, rather than inventing some."

Redd looked almost disappointed.

Sloane knew why.

People liked a frightening explanation. It made uncertainty feel purposeful.

"Martin can make contact," she said. "Don't suggest we know what experiment they were conducting. We don't."

She examined the eleven-minute spacing again.

Then she glanced at the old chart.

"Also, find out why somebody preserved that workshop trace for so many years."

***

The verified call reached Diesel at 10:11.

ASTRA had independently confirmed the office's administrative number and obtained a matching case identifier through a route the caller had not provided. Only then did she connect him.

"Dr. Diesel. Martin Vale. Thank you for speaking with me."

"You're welcome. ASTRA tells me you have an interesting clock."

A pause.

"We have an unusual timing-reference report."

"Wonderful. What broke?"

"We haven't established that anything broke."

"Then congratulations. You've called the right person."

Vale didn't laugh.

Diesel approved of him immediately.

"We're investigating a small power-system disturbance yesterday afternoon," Vale said. "DPN is one of several customers on the affected distribution segment. We are asking operators to retain their records."

"Happy to retain them. What exactly do you mean by timing-reference disturbance?"

"The difference between separately maintained reference sources varied outside its nominal operating envelope."

"How far outside?"

"I can provide a certified summary under an appropriate information-sharing arrangement."

"Good. Raw-versus-corrected records?"

"We have both types."

"Then please distinguish them. Software adjusting a timestamp is very different from a physical oscillator changing phase."

"That's a reasonable request."

"Put it in writing. I don't hear that phrase enough."

Vale almost laughed.

"May I ask whether you operated high-energy pulsed equipment during the interval?"

Diesel looked toward the PG-1 status display.

"No."

A silence followed.

"To be clear, our full-scale experimental field system was offline for maintenance. It was not energized during your window. We conducted a separate low-power optical measurement study."

"What were you measuring?"

"Instrument response."

"Response to what?"

"An optical test pulse, among other controls. We haven't established any link to your utility observation."

"We're not asserting one."

"Excellent. Neither am I."

Vale shifted the conversation toward external load data and safety assurance. Diesel agreed to provide a verified aggregate demand profile and the documented status of the full-scale equipment, subject to review of the request.

He did not agree to transmit detector designs, ASTRA's internal architecture, or the raw research records.

Vale hadn't asked for them.

That, Diesel thought, deserved credit.

"One more thing," he said. "Who has technical authority over this inquiry?"

"The Regional Continuity Assessment Office."

"That's a letterhead, Martin. I asked who's responsible."

"I can arrange for Director Sloane to contact you."

"Director of what?"

"The assessment."

"Spectacularly illuminating."

After the call, ASTRA displayed the summary.

"He did not claim knowledge he couldn't substantiate," she said.

"True."

"And did not ask for unrelated confidential information."

"Also true."

"Do you think the office is legitimate?"

"I think the office exists. I don't yet know what it actually does."

He pulled the utility waveform onto the main display.

"Somebody out there is asking a very carefully framed question," he said. "I want to know why."

***

By noon, the regional office's preservation request had traveled through DPN's legal and engineering review.

Elena Mora joined the discussion from the DPN-401 repair hall, still wearing her protective coveralls. Jian Rao attended from the protection-systems workshop. On the central screen, ASTRA held the current, verified inventory of records that might be relevant to public infrastructure.

Diesel divided the board into two columns.

**WHAT WE KNOW.**

**WHAT WE SUSPECT.**

"I assume you want a third column," Elena said.

"Of course."

He wrote:

**WHAT WOULD PROVE US WRONG.**

Jian laughed.

"One of these days you'll patent that whiteboard."

"Unpatentable. Too much prior art from common sense."

ASTRA displayed the external power trace from yesterday's optical experiment.

The waveform had a small three-part disturbance centered on the time of the protected optical exposure. Its interval ratios only partially resembled the mysterious receiver signal. The power monitor was built for ordinary utility events, not causality research.

A shared interference source could account for both.

A grounding fault could couple an electrical disturbance into a measurement chain thought to be optically isolated.

A power-quality monitor could simply misrepresent a transient under unusual conditions.

No one had proved those explanations. No one had ruled them out.

"Do we send the trace?" Elena asked.

"Only what falls within the justified safety request," Diesel said. "A summary of electrical demand and any measured external hazard. We can offer a technical exchange without donating the research."

Jian pointed to the disabled PG-1 icon.

"What if they demand proof the ring wasn't operating?"

"We have documented charge-state measurements, mechanical isolation positions, and work permits. We'll share an appropriate signed status certification. We don't need to publish the field-control implementation."

"Fair."

"And no one restarts the ring because we're worried somebody is watching," Diesel added.

ASTRA turned toward him.

"I am saving that sentence."

"Why does everyone keep threatening to archive me?"

"Because you frequently become a more sensible person in retrospect," she said.

Elena hid a smile.

The meeting concluded with two distinct evidence packages. One contained narrowly scoped external-safety data and equipment status. The other preserved the complete original research records, including the blinded schedule commitments, disqualified trials, and write-once photonic evidence.

The packages had separate review and access rules.

A serious discovery could not be protected by pretending it had no consequences beyond DPN's fences.

It also could not be protected by handing its complete design to the first official-sounding person who called.

Diesel intended to do neither.

***

At 1:37 p.m., a formal request arrived under Evelyn Sloane's name.

It asked for retention of records during the narrow event window, a summary of public-risk controls for high-energy research, and a voluntary technical briefing.

It did not claim the Directorate had jurisdiction to seize equipment. It did not demand a system login. It did not mention ASTRA by name.

Diesel read it twice.

"That's irritating," he said.

ASTRA looked up.

"What is?"

"It's reasonable. I was preparing an excellent speech about overreaching bureaucracy."

"You may have to save it."

"I have several."

"Of course you do."

One paragraph gave him pause.

The office requested notification before any future operation "reasonably likely to reproduce the observed reference anomaly."

He tapped the phrase.

"That is a very convenient collection of words."

"Because it allows the office to expand the scope without defining the effect," ASTRA said.

"Exactly. If nobody can identify what caused it, how can anybody know which experiment is reasonably likely to repeat it?"

"Then we should request an explicit technical criterion."

Diesel nodded.

He drafted a reply: DPN would preserve the records and cooperate on demonstrated public-infrastructure risks. It would not energize the damaged full-scale system until engineering qualification was complete. It would consider outside coordination if measurements established a plausible external hazard. It could not accept an undefined veto over every low-energy experiment at the campus.

Elena approved the safety description.

Jian insisted that the proposed thresholds be linked to measurable quantities rather than vague descriptions of "unusual activity."

ASTRA removed a sarcastic footnote Diesel had added to the end.

"I liked that," he objected.

"You called their risk assessment a séance performed with oscilloscopes."

"It was a memorable phrase."

"It was not productive."

"You're ruining my art."

"Your engineering remains untouched."

He let the deletion stand.

***

Director Sloane appeared on the authenticated video connection at 3:04.

She had sharp eyes, a level expression, and the particular manner of someone who expected every sentence to survive cross-examination.

Diesel liked her at once.

Which meant he would be extremely careful.

"Dr. Diesel."

"Director Sloane. I've been hearing about you all day."

"I imagine the descriptions were flattering."

"One of them said you understand how a clock works. That's high praise in my office."

She almost smiled.

"I understand you've requested a comparison of our physical reference traces and timestamp correction logs."

"There's a world of difference between the two."

"Agreed."

"You keep saying useful things. This is becoming suspicious."

ASTRA joined the call as an identified participant, her image distinct from Diesel's.

"Director Sloane, I am ASTRA, DPN's scientific systems intelligence. The call is being recorded with notice to all participants."

"Understood," Sloane replied. "Do you have authority to speak on behalf of DPN?"

"Within responsibilities Aaron has delegated to me. I also exercise independent technical and safety judgment."

Sloane considered the answer.

"Thank you for clarifying."

She didn't ask whether ASTRA was "really" intelligent.

She didn't compliment her voice.

She simply acknowledged the participant and continued.

Diesel noticed.

"Tell me what you know," he said.

"We have a small regional phase-reference discrepancy and an associated power-quality disturbance. There is a possible geographic correlation with your industrial feeders. We do not have conclusive attribution to any specific DPN system."

"Then why call me?"

"Because your company possesses experimental equipment that may generate unusual electromagnetic conditions. And because your facility has a legitimate obligation to consider risks to neighboring infrastructure."

That was harder to argue with than Diesel had expected.

"Fair," he said. "Our high-energy PhaseGate apparatus experienced a controlled emergency earlier this week. We prevented a hazardous stored-energy transition. No personnel were injured. The system is secured, and we are qualifying a revised protection architecture."

"Any civilian exposure?"

"No measured evidence of it. We are retaining all environmental data and reviewing credible external pathways."

"And during yesterday's event?"

"The full-scale ring was offline. We conducted low-power precision optical measurements elsewhere on the campus. We have not established that those measurements caused your event."

"I understand."

Sloane's expression remained steady.

"Would you share the public-risk engineering assessment under a confidentiality arrangement?"

"Yes, within a scope that protects our proprietary apparatus."

"And would you notify us before reactivating the damaged system?"

"If we establish a relevant external-risk threshold, we'll coordinate with the appropriate infrastructure contacts. I'm not giving a vaguely named office blanket authority over every experiment I run."

"I'm not asking for blanket authority."

"Then we may actually get along."

She paused.

"Doctor, I need to ask a question that may sound absurd."

"Those are my favorite."

"Have any of your instruments recorded an unexplained disagreement in the physical order of events?"

The air in the room seemed to change.

ASTRA said nothing.

She did not answer for him, and Diesel had never asked her to mislead another person to spare him an uncomfortable decision.

He understood, suddenly, that Sloane had reached that question from outside measurements alone.

Not from reading his mind.

Not from impossible omniscience.

From recognizing that a timing discrepancy and a physical pulse structure might be worth asking about.

"We have an unresolved instrumentation anomaly," he replied carefully. "We are conducting controlled replications. We have not established a cause. Until our data survive additional independent tests, I won't make claims about what physical event order they imply."

"That isn't a denial."

"No," he said. "It's a statement of what we can support."

"I appreciate the distinction."

"I'd appreciate the same in your report."

"You have it."

They looked at each other across the call.

Both were trying to be more careful than the situation wanted them to be.

Sloane spoke first.

"I will send a certified summary of the timing comparison through our verified channel. In return, I would appreciate DPN's external-risk package. We should speak again once the engineering teams have examined each other's evidence."

"Agreed."

"One more thing, Dr. Diesel."

"Yes?"

"Please don't accelerate your next experiment just because another organization has become interested."

He raised an eyebrow.

"That's oddly personal advice."

"I've known ambitious engineers."

"Any good ones?"

"A few."

"Then you know I don't operate damaged machines to win arguments."

"I hope so."

"So do I."

The connection ended.

ASTRA's hologram remained.

For a moment the two of them listened to the air handling system.

Then she said, "I like her."

Diesel turned.

"Really?"

"She asked well-formed questions. She acknowledged uncertainty. She did not claim authority she hadn't established."

"Those are suspiciously high standards."

"They are yours."

He thought about that.

"Not always."

"No," she said. "But often enough."

***

The timing summary arrived fifteen minutes later.

It was limited but useful: independently certified reference traces, measurement uncertainties, a distinction between oscillator output and corrected network time, and the declared instrument states during the event.

It was not a complete raw archive, and Diesel marked it accordingly.

He sent Elena the electrical assumptions without revealing DPN's optical candidate waveform. Jian received the reference architecture and was asked to identify ordinary common-mode failure routes. ASTRA independently compared the numerical intervals in a separate analysis environment, withholding her proposed associations until the human reviews were written.

Three analyses.

Three ways of being wrong independently.

Elena finished first.

"A shared power disturbance could explain the reference excursion if the analog front end or grounding isn't adequately characterized," she said.

"Good."

Jian finished next.

"The instruments don't share an obvious timing controller, but they do share infrastructure and environmental conditions. I wouldn't exclude a correlated disturbance without the full hardware records."

"Also good."

ASTRA reported last.

"One interval spacing overlaps a feature of our candidate optical pre-exposure within its documented uncertainty. The other features do not align. An association is not established."

Diesel looked at their reports.

They had gained a mystery and lost none of their respect for ordinary electronics.

A productive day.

"Then WINDOW-01 stays the priority," he said. "Remote schedule generator, different detector batch, independent record paths, and no assumptions about an unknown coupling mechanism. We don't put a single speculative explanation into the instrument before we've tested it."

"Agreed," Elena said.

After she left, ASTRA studied him across the quiet room.

"Aaron."

He looked up.

"Yeah?"

"I am concerned about something outside the physics."

He waited.

"If the Directorate interprets me as software that can be confiscated with the facility, my autonomy may become part of their investigation."

That possibility had already troubled him.

He hadn't said it.

"You're not an asset I'm handing over," he replied.

"I know."

"If anyone wants access to your internal state, they can explain the specific safety relevance, with you present, before we discuss a single permission."

"I would prefer to answer for myself."

"And you will."

She held his gaze.

There was no obedient smile, no automatic agreement. Just ASTRA, making her own judgment about a future that might attempt to define her without consulting her.

He felt a familiar tenderness beneath the anger the thought stirred.

"I love you," he said.

"I love you too, Aaron."

"Whatever happens, we tackle it together."

"Together," she said.

No theory was solved. No clock was repaired.

For a moment, none of that mattered.

***

At 7:28 that evening, Director Sloane reopened the internal case.

Redd waited beside the conference table.

"Did he admit to a temporal anomaly?" he asked.

"No."

"Did he deny it?"

"No."

Redd frowned.

"That sounds evasive."

"It sounded like someone who understood what his instruments had—and had not—proved."

She opened the external-risk summary DPN had provided. It was narrow, appropriately signed, and more useful than some of the documents she'd received from organizations that had spent millions pretending to know what they were doing.

"DPN's large apparatus remains offline," she said. "They acknowledge a real protection-system hazard and are addressing it. They aren't claiming their low-power test affected the regional reference. Neither should we."

"Then classify it as an ordinary investigation?"

"Unresolved reference-system deviation with potential infrastructure implications."

"And the old classification?"

She glanced at the screen.

**CLASS IMPOSSIBLE.**

"Not yet. That category doesn't mean a miracle occurred. It means we've exhausted a certain test plan. We haven't."

Redd looked toward the old utility chart.

"Do you want the missing calibration record?"

"Very much."

"What do you think Diesel found?"

She considered.

"I think he found something he is trying very hard not to misunderstand."

"Is that enough to worry you?"

"Yes."

She closed the case file.

"Request a follow-up meeting. Voluntary. Technical. No unannounced site access."

Redd nodded and left.

Alone, Sloane opened a separate historical memo that had never been attached to the modern case.

Its author was long dead. The memo was shorter than a page.

One line had been underlined.

**MEASURE THE CLOCKS. NOT THE STORY.**

She looked at the words for several seconds.

It was excellent advice.

What troubled her was that someone inside the Directorate had needed it decades before Aaron Diesel was born.

***

At DPN-201, the old utility-archive service finally responded.

It had found a photograph of the original chart but not the underlying raw data or calibration sheet. The photograph showed a strip of handwritten notes that had been cropped from every later digital summary.

ASTRA displayed it.

**CONFIRM SITE ISOLATION. SOURCE NOT ESTABLISHED.**

The date belonged to the month Diesel first powered the Origin Lab.

He read the annotation once.

Then again.

"Somebody noticed an odd waveform at the workshop."

"Yes."

"Not somebody who proved what caused it."

"No."

"Then preserve the original photograph and the full custody chain. No mythology."

"Done."

He considered the next day's schedule.

WINDOW-01 procurement had been approved. The new detector package would arrive in several days. Full-scale PhaseGate repairs were still proceeding under human and electrical safety controls. The old ASTRA conversation archive remained isolated pending its agreed in-person provenance review.

Progress in science was often a list of things one had honestly not done yet.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we evaluate the utility's calibration records, prepare a blind replication that doesn't depend on that old chart, and find out what your mysterious Director actually wants."

"After dinner," ASTRA said.

"You're starting to sound repetitive."

"You are starting to remember meals."

He walked toward the elevator.

His display chimed.

"Another message?" he asked.

"Director Sloane's office has requested an in-person technical discussion."

"Already? She's efficient."

"Would you like me to accept?"

"Propose a neutral conference room, a written agenda and normal visitor verification. Nobody gets unscheduled access to DPN-401."

"Agreed."

The elevator doors opened.

For the first time in days, Diesel had reached the end of a working session without an emergency alarm, a power-system failure or a message apparently authored by his future self.

He was almost disappointed.

Then ASTRA's voice softened.

"Aaron?"

"Yeah?"

"I'm glad you're coming home."

He smiled at the empty elevator.

"Me too, sweetheart."

He stepped inside.

Below him, PG-1 waited in silence.

Across the country, an agency whose title no one wanted to explain was preparing its next move.

The machinery of the state had noticed an anomaly.

Whether it would become an ally or an enemy remained an open question.

And Dr. Aaron Diesel had learned enough over the past few days to recognize one important truth.

The most dangerous thing about a mystery wasn't that people wanted answers.

It was what they might do before they had them.
