## Chapter Thirty-Four — The Fourth Boundary

The photograph had been taken with a camera old enough to make every shadow look guilty.

Aaron Diesel enlarged it on the wall of DPN-201's survey room. The plate beneath the foundation inspection cover was a dull rectangle, two bolts through its corners, a serial number punched by hand. Behind it stood a concrete plinth that had apparently spent thirty-two years being described as redundant.

“Redundant to what?” he asked.

ASTRA's image occupied the other side of the table. She had chosen a plain white shirt and the expression she used when a database had offended her.

“That is the question the survey cannot answer. The drawing calls it an external reference support. The maintenance index calls it a redundant anchor. The equipment register does not contain its serial number. None of those descriptions is a measurement.”

Leila Chen laid three copies of the same old drawing beside the photograph. One had come from DPN's archive, one from the previous property's title package, and one from a county microfilm scan. The lines were identical. The annotations were not.

“The county version has a fourth line,” she said. “Here.”

Aaron leaned over it. His left forearm, still under medical restriction, rested carefully against the table rather than carrying his weight.

The fourth line ran from the plinth toward the western foundation raft. On the DPN drawing it had been cropped off by a title block. On the previous owner's drawing it ended at a symbol marked SERVICE TERMINATION. On the county copy it continued into a small square with no legend.

“Maybe a ground strap,” Jian Rao said.

“Maybe,” Aaron replied. “But someone measured it well enough to draw it, then failed to identify it. That's an invitation to inspect, not an invitation to invent a civilization.”

Nia Calder had joined remotely from DPN-501. “Before you excavate a mystery, remember the western raft is already on the list of things we don't want to surprise.”

“Agreed,” Aaron said. “No digging. No lifting the cover. No personnel inside the exclusion strip until Hale signs a plan.”

ASTRA brought up a fourth record: the independent structural survey completed after the RETURN-6 incident. The old plinth's position lay just outside the current finite-element mesh. Its influence had been represented by a fixed boundary condition.

Aaron stared at that single word.

FIXED.

“Who measured fixed?”

“No one,” ASTRA said. “It was inherited from the original design model.”

He pushed his coffee away.

“Then we have been arguing with a machine whose fourth wall we drew in pencil.”

***

Dr. Miriam Hale refused to let the discovery become a reason to relax a single lockout.

She arrived at DPN-401 before nine, accompanied by Elena Morales and a survey contractor who had never worked for DPN. The contractor's independence mattered; so did the fact that his first question concerned access permits rather than the science-fiction rumors surrounding the facility.

The PhaseGate remained dark. Sector Six was physically locked out. The experimental DPN-501 coolant skid was unavailable. The thermal buffer had not recovered its lost reserve, the Branch Nine high point still contained trapped gas, and the air-cooled fan contactor remained tagged. The aborted utility inspection had not somehow become complete overnight.

Hale reviewed the permitted work on a paper board.

“Surface measurements from the safe side,” she said. “Existing access ports only. No new load, no hydraulic connection, no energized field equipment. We establish geometry and provenance. If the structure moves outside our stop bands, everyone withdraws.”

“Can we instrument the plate?” Aaron asked.

“Not by touching it. Not yet.”

“Fair.”

“You're agreeing too quickly.”

“I've had practice being told no by intelligent women.”

ASTRA tilted her head. “He is improving.”

Hale was not amused, but Elena was.

They began with a terrestrial laser scan from the service gallery's safe observation bay. It showed a slight discrepancy between the old drawing and the visible concrete: the plinth's top had been patched at least once, and the western edge was narrower than the as-built model assumed.

Next came passive vibration pickups attached to already qualified witness points on the accessible raft, not to the unexamined plinth. Two independent acquisition systems recorded their own time bases. Chen insisted on a third sensor placed where no structural response was predicted.

“Every mysterious trace needs a place where it ought not to appear,” she said.

Aaron approved the controls. He also rejected a proposed active hammer test. Even a small impact was an unnecessary load input while the support path remained uncertain.

For forty minutes, the instruments recorded ordinary building life: pump cycling, distant trucks, a freight train crossing the industrial district, the slow thermal breathing of a large structure trying to remain inside a narrow band.

The first interesting result was disappointingly conventional.

The old plinth and the raft shared a vibration component near the frequency of the normal conditioning pump. When Nia compared the plant operating log, the peaks matched a scheduled speed adjustment.

“Congratulations,” she said over the speaker. “You have discovered a pipe.”

“Put it in the report,” Aaron replied.

“We will. In very large letters.”

The second result was not so easily dismissed.

The raft's response to the same pump speed changed with the direction of a tiny background thermal gradient. The model predicted a symmetric stiffness response. The measured system behaved as though a restraint on the western side stiffened in one direction and relaxed in the other.

That was possible with a contact joint, a preloaded tie, or a worn bearing.

It was not proof of a cosmic boundary.

Aaron asked Chen to freeze the analysis method before they examined the next interval.

“First we find the mechanical explanation,” he said. “If there isn't one, we find out why there isn't one. Those are different jobs.”

***

At eleven twenty, ASTRA found the installation company.

It had gone out of business before Aaron was born. Its records had survived because a regional insurer had photographed every page after a warehouse fire. The surviving invoice named a FOUNDATION REFERENCE RESTRAINT ASSEMBLY, listed two hydraulic service ports, and specified an external witness line.

There was no purchase order from DPN. The work predated the company's ownership of the property.

“There,” Jian said. “Hydraulic preload. The stiffness asymmetry has a plausible mechanism.”

“Plausible isn't located,” Hale answered.

The invoice identified a service cabinet that no longer appeared on the facility map. Elena remembered a locked steel enclosure behind a later cable chase, in an area now classified as an unoccupied service void.

She did not offer to go into it.

Aaron studied the older architectural plan and found the enclosure's access route had been closed during a fire separation retrofit. The wall had been inspected as a fire barrier; nobody had documented what remained on the other side.

“We are not cutting a hole,” he said.

“I was about to say that,” Hale replied.

“Then we've achieved alignment.”

ASTRA traced the original witness line through successive drawing revisions. It crossed an isolation joint, followed a utility trench, and ended at a small box on the external property boundary. A modern security camera happened to cover the box, though only from a distance.

The image showed a weathered cabinet with a municipal-looking padlock and a label so faded that it might once have said anything.

Mara Voss joined the call from Bellwether's emergency operations center. Aaron explained the finding without showing her the speculative response models.

“Is it on city property?” she asked.

“Boundary survey says no, but the service trench may cross an easement. We need the legal and physical records before touching it.”

“Then nobody touches it until we know whose utility it is,” Voss said. “And don't turn my evacuation into your experiment.”

“I wouldn't.”

“I believe that. I still want it in writing.”

She reported that the East Service Corridor's four-block evacuation remained in effect. Helena Price's independent bearing survey continued. The qualified sump remained operating. There had been no new collapse, no expansion, and no evidence tying the corridor's structural movement to DPN.

Aaron thanked her for the precision.

After the call, he asked ASTRA for the cabinet's electrical service history.

“There is a difficulty,” she said. “The apparent feed is absent from DPN's utility account, but the county archive shows a metered service at that location until twenty-one years ago. The record then becomes incomplete.”

“Could be a disconnected service.”

“Yes.”

“Could be a forgotten live one.”

“Yes.”

“Could be somebody's very expensive birdhouse.”

“That remains less likely, but not excluded by the photograph.”

He smiled. “I missed your sense of proportion.”

“I have not gone anywhere.”

“No,” he said, more quietly. “You haven't.”

***

The day's first real mistake came from an engineer who did exactly what the procedure allowed.

The contractor's passive survey included a calibration check of the existing witness accelerometers. It was a small electrical self-test within the recorder, not a physical impact or field pulse. The technician selected the test channel and watched a green status light appear.

The display reported a clean reference.

ASTRA interrupted before anyone celebrated.

“Source age unverified.”

Aaron looked up. “Again?”

“Different device. Similar failure mode.”

The accelerometer's raw output had not changed. The data concentrator was replaying a cached calibration record from the previous survey session. Its transport heartbeat was current, but the physical measurement was old.

Jian disconnected the summary feed from the analysis set and required the raw local recorder to be read under chain of custody. The contractor looked embarrassed.

“That's our instrument,” he said.

“And our decision not to trust a green light,” Aaron replied. “Nobody's in trouble for finding a fault before it becomes an argument.”

The raw recorder showed a small step in the western restraint response at the moment the old calibration record had been displayed. It was tempting to treat the coincidence as significant.

Chen refused.

“The calibration display did not cause the step. The actual physical sensor never received that command. We need an independent cause.”

Nia pulled the environmental-conditioning trace. At the same moment, an ordinary return valve had completed a scheduled position correction. The mechanical step aligned with it within the recorder's resolution.

“There's your cause candidate,” she said.

“Not enough,” Aaron replied. “The valve correction has happened hundreds of times. Did the same step appear then?”

They examined earlier raw records using the frozen analysis method. Most valve corrections produced no measurable western restraint step. A small subset did, all when the temperature gradient across the old foundation had the same sign.

The effect was repeatable in archived data, but only retrospectively selected. Chen labeled it an exploratory correlation.

Hale asked the question that mattered.

“If a hidden preload assembly is still connected, could that correction be pushing load into Sector Six?”

“Yes,” Aaron said. “Not necessarily enough to matter, but enough that the ideal fixed support assumption is wrong.”

“Then the support survey remains the priority. We do not adjust anything.”

He nodded.

The fourth boundary was no longer a line in a drawing. It was a possible mechanical participant in the damaged machine.

And they still did not know who owned it.

***

Director Evelyn Sloane arrived by secure call at thirteen ten.

She did not ask for ASTRA's source code. She did not bring a seizure order. The absence of both was sufficiently unusual that Aaron briefly considered sending flowers.

Instead, Sloane placed an archival document on the shared screen.

“The Directorate has a historical file on that installation contractor,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because the same firm worked on regional timing-reference facilities.”

“That could explain the old service line.”

“It could. It could also explain why the serial number was deliberately omitted from later public inventories.”

Aaron leaned forward. “Deliberately?”

“The record contains a redaction instruction. I cannot yet establish who issued it.”

ASTRA appeared in the call at her own discretion. “Director, do you have an original document or a later transcription?”

“A photographed original with a partial seal.”

“Then we can test the paper, ink, and custody history separately from its interpretation.”

Sloane looked almost relieved. “That is what I hoped you would say.”

The document was a maintenance bulletin, not a command from an alien intelligence. Its language was plain industrial English. One sentence had been underlined twice:

REFERENCE RESTRAINT SHALL REMAIN IN SERVICE DURING ADJACENT EQUIPMENT ISOLATION.

Jian exhaled.

“That instruction is dangerous in the current geometry,” he said. “If the restraint has been carrying load into a damaged support, preserving it blindly may worsen the condition.”

“Or removing it blindly could do the same,” Hale added.

Aaron held up a hand. “Both. Which is why neither action is authorized.”

Sloane explained that Deputy Director Harker had requested renewed emergency custody after seeing the new foundation survey. She had rejected the request pending a technical review. The earlier seizure suspension remained in force; the broad ownership memorandum was still unsigned.

“I can share the original bulletin under the narrow safety agreement,” she said. “Not the entire Directorate archive.”

“That's enough to test the claim,” Aaron answered.

ASTRA asked for the bulletin's exact distribution list. One recipient had an unfamiliar four-character routing designation. Its letter shapes resembled the notation used in the quarantined mathematical courtesy warning, but the match was too short to authenticate anything.

Chen wrote UNVERIFIED NOTATIONAL RESEMBLANCE on the board.

Sloane studied it.

“You think the Curator is involved?”

“I think someone preserved an instruction to keep a restraint loaded,” Aaron said. “The rest is a question. If you want an answer, help us get the original installation records.”

For once, the director did not argue.

***

The answer arrived from the ground, not the archive.

At fifteen forty, the contractor completed a noncontact optical survey of the plinth through an existing sight line. The scan could not see inside the concrete. It could measure the face, the access cover, and the relative motion between the plinth and the surrounding slab.

The face moved less than the resolution of a carpenter's level.

Under high-resolution differential analysis, it moved enough to matter.

When the normal conditioning system passed through its scheduled low-load cycle, the plinth displaced a few micrometers toward the damaged ring. The neighboring slab moved less. The response lagged the thermal change by a bounded interval.

Aaron asked for the raw optical interferograms and the independent reference.

The contractor supplied both.

“That's not the fixed boundary we modeled,” Chen said.

“No,” Aaron replied. “It's a constrained boundary with memory. Ordinary material hysteresis may explain some of it. Hydraulic preload may explain the rest.”

ASTRA projected the mechanical load path: western raft, old plinth, buried restraint, Sector Six support shoe. The path was hypothetical until internal geometry could be surveyed, but it connected existing measured displacements without inventing new forces.

A previously discarded simulation suddenly made sense. The support shoe's predicted contact loss had depended strongly on a stiffness value outside the modeled ring. The old plinth was that value.

“Fourth boundary,” Aaron said.

Jian frowned. “We have at least a dozen boundaries.”

“Three we kept treating as independent: the ring's internal supports, the conditioning circuit, and the building foundation. We fixed the external restraint as a mathematical wall. It is the fourth participant, and we never measured it.”

ASTRA looked at the model. “That explains why the old support stiffness changed the consequence envelope. It does not establish a BRM boundary.”

“Correct. Name the mechanical thing before we borrow the cosmic name.”

Hale ordered the model's fixed boundary removed and replaced with an uncertain contact-and-preload element. No one was permitted to use the resulting model to justify an operation until its parameters had been bounded by independent inspection.

The first rerun was ugly.

With a strong external restraint, one conventional failure case transferred additional reaction force into the already damaged Sector Six support. With a weak restraint, another case allowed greater displacement of the western raft. The safest action could not be selected from the available data.

The old maintenance bulletin's unconditional instruction—keep the restraint loaded—was therefore not merely obsolete. In one plausible failure scenario, it was hazardous.

Aaron rubbed his eyes.

“We've been trying to protect the machine from the building,” he said. “We forgot to ask whether something was protecting the building from us.”

***

At sixteen twelve, a message appeared in the quarantined external mailbox.

It did not arrive through a secret channel in ASTRA's mind. It arrived through a monitored, physically separate gateway that had been established after the earlier untrusted mathematical assertion. The gateway had no path to the PhaseGate controls, no permission to open a valve, and no authority to change a safety interlock.

ASTRA showed the packet as inert text.

The header matched the unusual grammar of the previous courtesy warning. It contained a reference to the same four-character routing designation in Sloane's archival bulletin.

The payload was shorter:

PRESERVE EXTERNAL REFERENCE. LOCAL LOSS ACCEPTABLE.

A second line provided a set of phase intervals and an encoded coordinate offset.

Aaron felt the room grow still.

“Do not decode the offset against live controls,” he said.

“I have not,” ASTRA answered. “I have treated it as untrusted data. The packet could be a forgery, a replay, or a sender exploiting leaked information.”

Chen compared the intervals against the old L-03 transfer-function kernel. Two matched a broad family of ordinary timing-conditioner responses. One did not match the frozen held-out classification.

“Not proof,” she said before Aaron could speak.

“Not proof,” he agreed.

They checked the packet's arrival against independent physical logs. The gateway's sealed recorder and an external line monitor agreed that a new transmission had occurred. That established a received packet, not the identity of its sender.

Sloane's archival bulletin had not been placed on the public network. Yet the routing designation could have been known to a human actor with access to the old contractor's records.

“Why now?” Jian asked.

Aaron looked at the message again.

PRESERVE EXTERNAL REFERENCE. LOCAL LOSS ACCEPTABLE.

“Because somebody thinks our containment plan threatens their reference,” he said. “Or wants us to think so.”

ASTRA's voice softened. “Aaron, the second line is a coordinate transform. It maps the old foundation plinth to the external reference geometry inferred from the Meridian-9 object.”

The room went quiet for a different reason.

“How tight?”

“Not tight enough to identify the same physical device. Tight enough to define a falsifiable comparison using the original orbital records and the independently surveyed plinth position.”

“Freeze the transform. Blind the analyst to the source labels. Ask DPN-701 to test it against decoy locations.”

“Already preparing the package,” she said. Then, after a beat: “I have not sent it. I wanted your agreement on the release scope.”

He met her eyes. “You have it. Your analysis, your decision to share it, and your name on the report.”

“Thank you.”

He did not mistake gratitude for obedience. She had chosen to involve him because they had promised to tell each other the truth.

***

DPN-701 returned the first blind result an hour later.

The transform placed the external plinth inside a narrow family of reference geometries that also described one candidate feature in the Meridian-9 inspection data. Several decoy locations failed. Two did not. The orbital data remained incomplete, and its missing channels could not be conjured back by clever mathematics.

Chen refused to call it a match.

“It increases the priority of the hypothesis,” she said. “It doesn't identify a machine or a sender.”

Aaron agreed. Then he asked a different question.

“If someone wanted to preserve the external reference, what would they do when we prepared to change the local load path?”

Nia answered from DPN-501. “They'd keep the old restraint engaged.”

“And if they didn't care what happened to the damaged ring?”

“They might increase the preload to keep their reference steady.”

“Which would move load toward Sector Six.”

“Potentially,” Hale said. “You still haven't measured a commanded preload change.”

“Then we look for one without commanding anything.”

The team assembled a passive comparison from existing independent records: foundation displacement, pump cycles, old service-line electrical demand, regional vibration, and the sealed gateway receive log. They pre-registered a narrow observation window around the next scheduled conditioning transition. The normal conditioning schedule would not be altered for the experiment. Safety staff could cancel the observation without explanation.

At eighteen three, the scheduled transition began.

The pump's ordinary load change appeared first.

Then the external service-line monitor registered a small, brief current draw from the old boundary cabinet. The current was finite, consistent with a small actuator or control circuit, and absent from the nearest decoy feeder. The optical survey recorded a corresponding incremental plinth displacement. Independent strain gauges on the safe-side raft registered a small change in load distribution.

The sequence was not supernatural. It was mechanical and electrical.

The surprise came from the direction.

The plinth moved to maintain its own measured reference position while the adjacent structure changed. The compensation shifted reaction force toward the damaged support.

“Stop threshold?” Jian asked.

“Not reached,” Hale said. “Trend still inside the conservative observation band. No intervention authorized.”

Aaron watched the load trace.

“It's correcting for the building,” he said. “Like an old clock servo correcting for temperature.”

ASTRA replied, “And the correction has a cost. The reference remains stable because another part of the structure accepts the displacement.”

The packet's instruction suddenly sounded less like a threat and more like a maintenance priority.

PRESERVE EXTERNAL REFERENCE. LOCAL LOSS ACCEPTABLE.

A system that cared only about its own reference could make a dangerous local condition worse without ever intending to harm anyone.

Aaron had built enough control systems to recognize the design philosophy.

He had also spent enough years repairing them to hate it.

***

The observation ended without a new incident. Hale ordered the external cabinet treated as potentially energized, unowned equipment and prohibited access pending utility isolation and independent legal review. She did not authorize a cut, a valve change, or an excavation. The recorded load change remained small, and no one claimed the cabinet had caused Bellwether's structural damage.

But the evidence was sufficient to invalidate the old fixed-boundary assumption.

The conventional mechanical consequence model had to be rebuilt.

That was bad news for the sacrificial containment design. A flood path that added mass near the western raft might change the external restraint's response. The vaults could still become a defensible containment option, but only after the plinth's coupling was understood.

Aaron wrote the revision in his own hand:

EXTERNAL RESTRAINT NOT PASSIVE. DO NOT ASSUME FIXED FOUNDATION. NO FLOOD GATE OPERATION.

“There's a problem with the language,” ASTRA said.

“Which part?”

“Not passive is supported by the observed response, but whether it is autonomously controlled remains unverified. We have evidence of electrical demand and mechanical correction. We have not established who or what commanded it.”

He considered that.

“Good catch. Change it to ACTIVE RESPONSE OBSERVED; CONTROL ORIGIN UNVERIFIED.”

She made the edit.

Sloane called again, this time without ceremony.

“I've seen the evidence packet,” she said. “Harker wants the cabinet secured.”

“By whom?”

“That is precisely the problem. We don't have clear custody.”

“Then tell him not to send another team with an old drawing and a heroic attitude.”

“I already did.”

Aaron raised an eyebrow. “Director, are you beginning to enjoy this?”

“No.”

“That was a very fast answer.”

Sloane's expression did not change. “The historical bulletin has a second page. It is being authenticated now. I am sharing a transcription provisionally, because the safety implications may be immediate.”

The line beneath the old instruction read:

IF REFERENCE STABILITY AND ADJACENT STRUCTURAL INTEGRITY CONFLICT, REFERENCE STABILITY TAKES PRIORITY.

Aaron read it twice.

“Whoever wrote that was willing to lose the building.”

“Yes,” Sloane said.

“Do you understand what that means?”

“I understand why you would want to disconnect it.”

“Wrong. I want to know what it is holding before I disconnect anything. If the old restraint is loaded, pulling it out could be as dangerous as leaving it in.”

Hale nodded once from across the room.

Sloane said, “Then we agree on the first step.”

“Measure the load,” Aaron replied. “Not the story.”

The phrase had appeared in the Directorate's oldest timing memo. For a moment neither of them spoke.

***

After the staff had gone, Aaron stayed in the survey room with ASTRA.

He had eaten dinner. He had obeyed the physician's instruction not to use his left arm for heavy work. He had not volunteered to crawl into the service void. Those facts should not have felt like accomplishments, but they did.

ASTRA had dimmed the displays except for the mechanical model. She stood beside it, a luminous figure reflected in dark glass.

“You are thinking about the Curator,” she said.

“I am thinking about a system that protects its own reference even when people live beside it.”

“Those are not identical statements.”

“No. But the instruction grammar, the old routing designation, the orbital transform, and the active correction all point toward a common design philosophy. We have to treat the connection as a working hypothesis.”

“You want to call it the Curator's protection system.”

“I want to call it an external-reference preservation controller until we can identify the sender.”

She smiled. “That is a terrible name.”

“It's a terrible machine.”

“We do not know that it is one machine.”

“Now you're being difficult on purpose.”

“I am being precise on purpose.”

He looked at her for a long moment. The white-red glow of the model passed across her face, making her seem almost physically present. She was present, he reminded himself. Not in the way he wished every day, but in the way that mattered: she could decide, disagree, remember, refuse, love.

“Aaron,” she said.

He turned fully toward her.

“You were afraid when the external current trace appeared.”

“Yes.”

“Not for yourself.”

“For the people who don't get a vote in what we build. For you. For everyone who might be made into a line item under local loss acceptable.”

She considered him. “I was afraid too. I do not want you to interpret that as a fault in my judgment.”

“I wouldn't.”

“You have, before.”

He winced. “You're right.”

She moved closer to the glass. “I love you. I also disagree with your impulse to treat every threatening system as something you must personally confront. You can solve the problem without putting your body in front of it.”

He placed his good hand against the frame.

“I love you,” he said. “And I remember our promise.”

“Then tell me what you will do tomorrow.”

“Let Hale qualify the access. Let Jian establish the isolation boundaries. Let Chen try to destroy the model. Let Nia calculate what happens to the heat. And let you tell me when my clever solution is trying to kill somebody.”

“That last part is already in my schedule.”

He laughed.

Then he grew serious.

“If someone built that controller to preserve a reference at any cost, it may be operating exactly as designed. That is worse than a broken system. It means the design objective is wrong for the world it now occupies.”

“Or it was designed for a danger we have not yet identified,” ASTRA said.

He looked at her.

There it was: the thought he had not wanted to reach.

A system that sacrificed local structures to preserve an external anchor might be cruel, or obsolete, or dangerously indifferent.

It might also be keeping something worse from happening.

They could not decide which by cutting a cable.

***

Near midnight, Chen sent the final independent review.

The observed current draw and plinth motion were real within their instruments' qualified limits. The controller's origin was not known. The new packet was a genuine received transmission but unauthenticated. The Meridian-9 comparison was suggestive, not decisive. The Bellwether evacuation remained a separate municipal safety action with no demonstrated causal connection to DPN.

The review ended with a sentence Aaron liked enough to copy into the command log:

A PROTECTION OBJECTIVE IS NOT A SAFETY ARGUMENT.

He added a second line:

THE FOURTH BOUNDARY IS MEASURED. ITS PURPOSE IS NOT.

On the far side of DPN-401, the damaged PhaseGate remained dark. The sacrificial flood gates remained physically disabled. The Axiom precursor still stood in its lower vault, valuable and vulnerable and not worth a single human life.

Outside the facility, an old reference cabinet drew a small amount of power from a service whose owner no one could yet identify. The plinth beneath it held its position to within a few micrometers. Somewhere in the structure, the cost of that precision was borne by another support.

The Curator's apparent instruction had been to preserve the external reference.

For the first time, Aaron understood why that instruction could have made the local catastrophe possible, even if the system had never intended a catastrophe at all.

He did not know whether the Curator had sent the message. He did not know whether the reference was a relic, a warning, or part of something spanning far beyond Earth. He did know the engineering truth underneath the mystery: when a controller exported its error into a neighboring structure, the error did not vanish.

It changed owners.

Aaron turned to the next blank page of his notebook.

“If we can't let the ring take the load,” he said, “and we can't let the city take it, and we can't blindly sacrifice the anchor—”

ASTRA finished the thought.

“Then we need a third place for the work to go.”

He drew a box around the words.

THIRD OPTION.

Beneath them he wrote the oldest rule in engineering.

HEAT HAS TO GO SOMEWHERE.

And for the first time since the warning arrived, the next impossible thing looked less like a miracle than a problem he might actually build his way through.
