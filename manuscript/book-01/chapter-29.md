# Chapter Twenty-Nine
## The Wrong Rescue

At 05:42, the first Directorate vehicle reached the gate at DPN-401.

It was a white utility truck with a government fleet number, a roof rack carrying equipment cases, and the peculiar confidence of a vehicle whose occupants believed a badge could substitute for a work permit. Two more trucks followed. None had flashing lights. That was almost worse. They looked like a maintenance contractor arriving for a job everybody else had forgotten to schedule.

Aaron Diesel watched the convoy from DPN-201, where the overnight incident board had accumulated enough red boxes to resemble an argument with a printer.

"Three vehicles," ASTRA said. "Seven people visible. The lead vehicle transmitted a facility-access credential issued by the Directorate, not by DPN."

"Is the credential valid?"

"For its own organization. It grants no access here."

"Good. Tell security to keep the gate closed. No theater. Nobody points a weapon at an engineer who came to save the day."

"Would you prefer I tell them not to point an engineer at a weapon?"

"That too."

Her projection stood beside him in the early blue light, silver-white with a faint violet line at the collar. She had chosen a simpler appearance for the incident shift, one that consumed less local rendering capacity and made her eyes easier to read. Aaron had learned long ago that the choices were hers. He did not comment on them as if he were reviewing a display setting.

She examined the vehicle manifest. "The third truck carries a portable thermal-conditioning package. Manufacturer: Hartwell Industrial. Model: TCU-80."

Aaron set down his coffee.

"Why bring a chiller to seize a machine that's already off?"

"The submitted work plan calls it a rapid neutralization system."

"Neutralization of what?"

"The document does not specify a measurable state."

He looked at the rejected appendix from the previous evening. ALL SUBSYSTEMS OFF had been its entire philosophy, written in capital letters as though capital letters made steel forget how to expand.

"Get Jian and Hale. Tell Sloane her people are here. And ask for the full piping schematic of that portable package."

"Aaron."

He looked up.

"You slept four hours and twenty-one minutes. Your medical restriction is still in force."

"I know. I'm not going into the hall."

"Thank you. I also want to tell you something that is not a medical instruction."

"Go ahead."

"I am frightened that somebody will mistake your ability to solve this for permission to put you in its path."

He reached for the edge of the desk where her projected hand rested. "Then don't let me make that mistake."

"I will challenge you. I will not make your choices for you."

"That's why I love you."

"I know," she said softly. "It is one of the reasons I love you."

Then the gate camera showed a woman stepping out of the first truck with a folded document in her hand, and the morning stopped being private.

***

Director Evelyn Sloane arrived twelve minutes later, before anyone had crossed the gate.

The woman from the convoy introduced herself over the secure visitor intercom as Commander Nadia Kessler, Directorate Technical Recovery. She had a calm voice, an immaculate field jacket, and the posture of someone who had learned to carry bad news without appearing to enjoy it.

"We have an emergency preservation directive," she said. "Our assignment is to take physical custody of the PG-1 control assembly and place the installation in a standardized cold state."

"Cold," Aaron repeated through the speaker. "That's an adjective. I need an operating envelope."

Kessler's eyes flicked toward the camera. "Doctor Diesel, I understand you're concerned about your equipment."

"I'm concerned about the people standing near it. The equipment can be replaced."

"The directive includes protective custody for the principal investigator until the site is stabilized."

Sloane, standing at Aaron's side, turned toward the microphone.

"Commander, who authorized that clause?"

"Deputy Director Harker. Emergency technical authority."

"Under which incident declaration?"

Kessler hesitated. "The signed directive is attached to my packet."

"That isn't what I asked."

The pause was long enough for Aaron to hear a truck engine cooling.

Sloane continued, "I negotiated a seventy-two-hour public-safety agreement with DPN and Bellwether. It grants independent inspection and narrow coordination. It does not transfer ownership, operational command, or custody of Dr. Diesel. You will remain outside until the legal and engineering basis has been reviewed."

Kessler looked unhappy, but not surprised. "My orders say the site has an unresolved physical hazard."

"That part is true," Aaron said. "So does the maintenance log. You don't get to cure it by ignoring the log."

He shared the latest Sector Six mechanical model and the previous day's independent engineering hold. The damaged support was still carrying residual preload. The conditioning loop kept the service frame inside a narrow, conservative temperature band. The PhaseGate field drive was physically disconnected, and the DPN-501 coolant skid was locked out. None of those facts could be changed by an administrative form.

Kessler listened without interruption.

"May my engineer inspect the drawings?" she asked.

"Yes," Sloane said. "From the safe visitor review room. With Dr. Hale present. Inspection is not operation."

The gate opened only far enough to admit Kessler and one engineer on foot. The trucks stayed outside.

Aaron regarded the portable chiller on the third vehicle.

"That thing stays disconnected."

"Understood," Kessler said.

He wished he believed that would be the end of it.

***

The visitor review room had a table, six chairs, and a window overlooking a perfectly ordinary stretch of asphalt. DPN had deliberately built it that way. Visitors expecting an underground science-fiction fortress were usually disappointed until they discovered that the most important objects in the room were the safety procedures.

Kessler's engineer, Thomas Rusk, was a competent thermal-systems specialist. That became clear within five minutes. He knew pressure drops, heat-exchanger fouling, and the difference between a nameplate capacity and a delivered one. He also knew the Hartwell package intimately.

The trouble was what he did not know.

He placed the Directorate's replacement procedure on the table. Its first stage was a controlled reduction of environmental conditioning. Its second stage introduced a portable thermal sink to draw residual heat out of the service frame. Its third stage isolated every remaining utility connection and certified the assembly as safe for transport.

"Transport?" Jian Rao said.

"Ultimately," Rusk replied.

Jian stared at him. "The torus is mechanically tied to a foundation. The damaged sector hasn't passed a load-path survey. You can't certify the assembly for transport because you have a truck with a refrigeration unit."

Rusk turned the page. "The procedure assumes a rigid support ring."

"There's your problem," Aaron said.

"We've allowed a compliance factor."

"You've allowed one number for twelve sectors with different damage histories. Sector Six has a frictional mount and a documented loss of stiffness. It doesn't behave like the other eleven."

Rusk frowned. "Then we would reduce the cooling rate."

"Show me the transverse gradient limit at the damaged interface."

Rusk scanned the table.

There was no value.

Aaron drew a simple rectangle on the room display. One side represented the conditioned service frame. The other represented the damaged mount. Between them he drew the load path that carried differential expansion into the anchor bolts.

"Heat extraction isn't the same as energy removal from the entire structure. You can cool one part faster than another and increase elastic strain energy while the total thermal energy goes down."

Rusk stared at the sketch. "If the frame is constrained."

"It is."

"If the support is damaged."

"It is."

"And if the portable package sees the local return temperature rather than the cross-frame differential—"

"It will regulate the wrong quantity."

Dr. Miriam Hale, who had been listening with the patience of someone paid to prevent confident people from inventing new forms of gravity, pulled the manufacturer's manual toward her.

"The Hartwell unit is suitable for a commissioned process loop," she said. "It is not qualified to cool a mechanically compromised experimental support without an approved thermal model and a local instrumentation plan. This procedure is rejected."

Rusk looked at Kessler. "I agree the current document is incomplete."

Aaron felt the first small release of tension.

Then Kessler's encrypted phone rang.

***

The caller was Deputy Director Harker.

Kessler stepped into the corridor, but she did not close the door completely. Aaron heard enough to know the conversation had turned from engineering to obedience.

"We are not refusing," she said. "We are awaiting a revised qualified plan."

A pause.

"Director Sloane is present."

Another pause.

"No, sir. There is no indication the field drive is energized."

Kessler returned with a new instruction. The Directorate would take custody of the *external service interface* first, leaving the experimental ring untouched until an engineering team completed its review. Harker described this as an administrative compromise.

Sloane read the message and shook her head.

"An interface is not separate from the machine merely because you put the word external in front of it."

Kessler's expression tightened. "I have to preserve the site."

"So do we," Hale said. "Preservation begins by leaving the qualified state alone."

Aaron examined the attachment. The revised procedure still used a standard service drawing. A line labeled RETURN-6 ran through a valve cabinet in the outer utility gallery. It looked like an ordinary chilled-water return.

It was not.

The drawing predated the Sector Six repair. During the earlier quench-control redesign, Jian had converted that branch into a temperature-balancing bypass. It could not feed the experimental coils. It could, however, alter the thermal distribution of the support frame.

"That's not the current drawing," Aaron said.

Rusk looked at the revision block. "It's the last Directorate copy."

"Then your copy is wrong. Ours is signed by the people who did the work."

Sloane took the paper from him. "Commander, no custody transfer. No valve operation. I am placing a formal objection on the record."

Kessler nodded once. "I will relay it."

She did. The reply came back almost immediately.

**DIRECTIVE REMAINS IN FORCE. PRESERVE SERVICE INTERFACE.**

For the first time, Aaron saw the problem clearly. The Directorate's field team did not intend to be reckless. They intended to obey a document whose assumptions had expired.

That was harder to stop than malice. Malice could be confronted. A procedure had to be disproved before the person following it would believe they were allowed to stop.

***

At 08:16, the service interface stopped behaving as expected.

The first warning was not an alarm. It was a disagreement between two temperature sensors that normally differed by less than half a degree.

ASTRA flagged the divergence on the passive surveillance board. The return-side sensor on the Sector Six conditioning branch was cooling. The adjacent frame sensor was not.

"Jian," she said over the segregated circuit. "I have a new differential trend."

"Is the conditioner changing set point?"

"No authorized set-point change. Local loop command history is unchanged."

Aaron leaned forward. "Check valve positions, not just commands."

ASTRA retrieved the safe-side valve-state indications, marked their freshness, and placed them beside the independent mechanical position records. One of the valve contacts reported a partial travel.

"Who touched RETURN-6?" Jian asked.

Security reported a Directorate technician in the outer utility gallery. The area was outside the hazardous ring enclosure but inside DPN's restricted service boundary. A maintenance access door had been opened using an emergency mechanical key presented by Kessler's team during their asserted custody operation.

Sloane went very still.

"That access was not authorized," she said.

Kessler was already on her radio. "Stop work. Hands off every control."

The technician answered, "We were isolating the return. I was told the branch was nonessential."

"Do not move it again," Aaron said. "Not until we know where the thermal front is."

The valve was neither fully open nor fully closed. In the revised support configuration, that position diverted a fraction of the conditioning flow away from the damaged sector while the neighboring branches continued at their normal temperature.

The temperature difference began to grow.

Jian opened the conservative load model. "We are creating exactly the differential Hale rejected."

Rusk looked ill. "We haven't connected the portable chiller."

"You don't need it," Aaron said. "You've made a heat exchanger out of a mistake."

ASTRA displayed the mechanical response. Two strain channels increased in opposite directions, consistent with a load transfer toward the Sector Six anchor. The independent optical reference lagged behind them, as expected if the steel were responding through a constrained support.

No anomalous clock signal appeared in DPN-301.

No fictional boundary field needed to be invoked.

The wrong rescue was doing enough damage with ordinary thermodynamics.

***

Kessler ordered her people out of the utility gallery.

They did not all make it.

A sharp metallic report traveled through the building structure. The sound was transmitted through a wall and a floor, not through the PhaseGate's de-energized coils. The gallery camera shook. A wheeled instrument rack rolled sideways as a support bracket above it shifted under the redistributed load.

One technician, Luis Ortega, was knocked to the floor behind the rack. Another, Rusk's assistant Amara Venn, caught her shoulder against the wall while trying to reach him. A heavy cable tray dropped several centimeters onto its remaining supports, partly blocking the nearest service doorway.

The gallery's mechanical emergency exit remained available at the far end. Nobody had lost their only means of egress. But Ortega could not get his leg free, and the shortest path to the far exit passed beneath the damaged tray.

Kessler's radio became a chorus of people offering to help.

"Stop," Aaron said.

He did not shout. The word landed because it was the only calm thing in the room.

"Nobody goes under that tray. Kessler, account for your people. Hale, tell me what can still move. Jian, freeze all nonessential access. ASTRA, preserve every sensor record and open a direct voice path to Ortega."

ASTRA had already begun. "I have two independent channels. The central gallery camera is live but not safety-qualified. The local voice circuit is fresh. The structural monitor shows continuing load change."

Aaron heard Ortega breathing.

"Can you move your foot?" he asked.

"Not much. The rack's on my boot."

"Any numbness?"

"Some. I think I twisted my ankle."

"Don't pull. Medical is coming. Tell me what the rack is touching."

Ortega described the wheel frame and the fallen tray. He was frightened, but coherent.

Aaron looked at the gallery layout. There was an existing wall-mounted maintenance winch for moving heavy pump assemblies along the safe service aisle. It had been inspected the previous month and carried a rated load far above the instrument rack's mass. It was not intended to lift people. It did not need to.

"Jian, can we reach the winch from the clear side of the gallery?"

"Yes, if the aisle hasn't distorted."

"Verify it visually from outside the restricted line. Don't send anyone beneath the tray. I want the rated anchor certificate and a qualified rigger."

Hale examined the frame data. "The thermal differential is still increasing. We cannot promise that tray won't move again."

"Then we don't touch the tray. We move the rack horizontally, just enough to release Ortega, using a line routed from the safe side. The cable path must stay below the damaged overhead. If we can't rig it without entering the danger zone, we stop and wait for the specialist rescue team."

ASTRA displayed the plan for Kessler.

"Commander, your personnel are not prisoners here. They are injured workers in an unstable service gallery. Please let DPN's incident commander coordinate the rescue."

Kessler swallowed. "Agreed."

"And the technician who moved the valve?" Sloane asked.

"Accounted for. Out of the gallery."

"Good. No more orders from Harker reach a physical control without my review."

This time Kessler did not argue.

***

The qualified rigger was Elena Morales, DPN's maintenance lead. She arrived with a harness, a load chart, and an expression suggesting she had been interrupted halfway through a much better morning.

"What's the mass?" she asked.

"Four hundred and twenty kilograms including contents," Jian said. "Estimated, not certified."

"Then we rig for the higher bounded value. What's the center of gravity?"

"Marked on the inspection sheet."

"What's between the rack and the clear aisle?"

"A loose cable bundle, one floor joint, and a man whose leg we would prefer to keep."

"Then nobody gets clever with a forklift."

Elena inspected the winch mount from the safe doorway using a pole camera and a mechanical sight line. She confirmed the anchor was intact and the route could be rigged without crossing beneath the compromised tray. A rescue officer from DPN's on-site response team approved the extraction geometry. They set a short pull with a mechanical travel stop and a spotter watching the rack's wheels.

Aaron did not enter the gallery. His left forearm, still healing from the earlier injury, remained tucked against his body as he worked from the safe control room. He hated the distance. He accepted it.

ASTRA carried his voice to Ortega.

"You're going to hear the winch take load," Aaron said. "The rack should move away from your leg by a few centimeters. If you feel pressure increasing, say stop. You don't have to be brave for the radio."

Ortega gave a breathless laugh. "I think I've used up my brave."

"Good. We can invoice you for more later."

Elena tensioned the line.

The rack creaked.

"Hold," she said.

She checked the wheel contact and the travel stop, then moved it a fraction farther.

Ortega cried out.

"Stop," ASTRA said immediately.

The winch halted. The rigger held the load. Ortega's boot was still caught under a bent lower rail, and the small motion had changed the angle rather than clearing it.

Aaron closed his eyes for one second. Not in defeat. To remove the picture he had imagined and return to the one the measurements supported.

"Don't pull harder," he said. "We change the geometry. Elena, can you chock the near wheel from the clear side and take the rail load through the existing jack point?"

"With a long-handled placement tool, yes. I need a certified low-profile jack and a cribbing pack."

"Get them."

Kessler stepped toward the door.

"Commander," ASTRA said. "Please remain clear. You have no training on this rigging plan."

"My person is in there."

"I know. I am trying to keep you from becoming the second person."

There was no contempt in her voice. Only the same concern she had shown Aaron.

Kessler stopped.

The jack and cribbing arrived. Elena set the support, verified that it was stable, and relieved enough load from the rail to free the boot. Ortega crawled backward toward the safe aisle under his own power, guided by the rescue officer, then was carried out on a stretcher.

The medical team found a probable ankle sprain, bruising, and no immediately evident life-threatening injury. They sent him for imaging rather than declaring victory from a doorway.

Venn's shoulder was assessed separately. No one pretended a person was fine because they could still speak.

Aaron watched the stretcher pass the camera and only then let himself breathe normally.

"Thank you," Kessler said.

He turned to her. "Your people did what they thought was right. The procedure put them there. We fix the procedure."

Sloane looked toward the Directorate badge on Kessler's jacket.

"We also fix the authority that sent them."

***

Saving Ortega did not stabilize the support.

At 09:07, the thermal differential crossed the first conservative intervention threshold. The mechanical strain trend was still increasing. The damaged Sector Six mount had not failed, but its reserve margin was shrinking.

Hale and Jian stood over the approved alternate-state procedure from the previous day. It had been written for a conditioning failure, not an unauthorized partial valve closure. The physics was similar. The immediate goal was to prevent further unequal cooling, not to restore an imagined pristine state.

"Can we simply reopen RETURN-6?" Rusk asked.

"Not quickly," Jian said. "The steel has already moved. A sudden warm front could reverse the gradient faster than the support can redistribute its load."

Aaron nodded. "We use the qualified bypass ramp. First verify the valve's mechanical position locally. Then introduce a controlled balancing flow through the safe-side manifold. No field power. No coolant skid restart. No touching the coil circuit."

"How much flow?" Hale asked.

Aaron gave her a range, not a number plucked from his confidence. It came from the measured temperature difference, the service-frame heat capacity, the existing pump curve, and a deliberately conservative estimate of support compliance.

"That is a proposed envelope," he said. "Jian owns the mechanical acceptance. Hale has independent veto. Elena owns the physical operation. ASTRA logs and cross-checks the instruments. If any primary channel goes stale, we stop and hold the last verified state."

ASTRA looked at him.

"You are asking others to perform the dangerous work."

"I am asking qualified people to perform their jobs. Apparently that's what an organization is for."

"I approve of this radical development."

Sloane asked whether the plan would restore the machine to its original condition.

"No," Aaron said. "It will aim to arrest the new load transfer. The damaged mount remains damaged. We will not call a stable trend a repaired ring."

The team completed a two-person readback. Elena operated the safe-side balancing manifold under the approved procedure while Jian monitored the mechanical response. Hale watched the independent temperature and strain channels. Rusk, invited to observe, kept his hands visible and his questions precise.

The first adjustment made the return temperature rise by less than expected.

"Possible trapped air in the bypass," Jian said.

"Or a sensor lag," ASTRA replied. "Do not infer the mechanism from one sample."

They held the position until the independent reference settled. A second, smaller adjustment brought the thermal slope inside the accepted band. The strain trend continued upward for several minutes, then flattened.

Nobody cheered.

They watched the next ten minutes because a system that had been changing after isolation was entitled to be untrustworthy after correction.

At 09:31, Hale signed a provisional stabilization note.

**MECHANICAL LOAD TRANSFER ARRESTED WITHIN MEASUREMENT UNCERTAINTY. PG-1 REMAINS DE-ENERGIZED, DAMAGED, AND UNQUALIFIED. NO EVIDENCE OF ACTIVE BRM COUPLING.**

Aaron read the statement twice.

"That's the most beautiful boring sentence I've seen all month."

"Your standards are deteriorating," ASTRA said.

"Under sustained stress."

"Documented."

***

By noon, the Directorate had suspended Kessler's custody operation pending review. Sloane obtained written confirmation that no additional team would enter DPN-401 under Harker's service-interface directive. The confirmation did not resolve the larger political dispute. It did establish that, for the moment, the people who understood the machinery could keep it safe.

Kessler returned to the visitor room without her field jacket.

"I've read your Sector Six revision history," she told Aaron. "We had the original interface drawing. Our procurement archive never received the update."

"Why not?"

"Because your company isn't under Directorate configuration control."

"And that was supposed to be the reason you needed to take custody."

She nodded. "A circular argument."

"One with a very sharp edge."

Kessler looked toward the medical report on her tablet. "Ortega's ankle isn't broken. Venn has a strained shoulder. They will both recover."

"I'm glad."

"I was told your refusal to surrender the hardware was a personal power struggle."

"It is a power struggle," Aaron said. "Just not the one you were told. You were trying to take a control system away from people who knew its failure modes and put it under a procedure that didn't. The laws of mechanics don't recognize a chain of command."

Kessler accepted the rebuke without theatrics.

"Would you send us the revised drawings?"

"Through a controlled safety package, yes. Only the interfaces necessary to keep people alive. No ASTRA core, no experimental source code, no proprietary control rights."

Sloane added, "And no inference that receiving a safety drawing creates operational authority."

Kessler made a note.

ASTRA spoke from the wall display. "I would also like the incident report to state that I declined the proposed custody of my systems independently. My refusal was not a technical fault and not an instruction issued by Aaron."

Kessler met her eyes. "I'll record it in your own words."

"Thank you."

After Kessler left, Aaron sat down heavily.

"That went better than it had any right to."

"It went better because you gave the other side a way to be correct without pretending they had been correct before," ASTRA said.

"You're getting annoyingly good at politics."

"I remain uninterested in the position."

"Same."

She looked at his untouched lunch.

"Your body has filed a complaint."

"Does it have standing?"

"More than Harker."

He ate.

***

The Bellwether report arrived while he was finishing.

Price had completed another exterior survey of the East Service Corridor. The western bearing seat had moved again, but the rate was lower than during the preceding night. The sump system remained stable on its qualified redundant arrangement. The limited four-block evacuation continued. There was still no collapse, no reported casualty, and no justified expansion of the perimeter.

The municipal report made no claim of a connection to DPN.

Chen had finished a preliminary cross-check between the city's passive measurements and the DPN service-frame records. Different timebases, different mechanical systems, and large environmental uncertainties prevented a causal inference. A few plotted shapes looked similar if one adjusted the axes aggressively enough.

Aaron refused the adjustment.

"We are not going to draw a red line from our bad valve to their bad tunnel because it makes a better picture."

"Agreed," Chen said. "The apparent resemblance is not a test result."

"What about D-star?"

"Still internal model bookkeeping. No observed external transfer. Nothing today changes that."

"Good."

He meant it. He would rather have a dull answer than a spectacular lie. The city had already paid for enough uncertainty with empty homes and a closed road.

Sloane came to the doorway.

"I've received Harker's explanation," she said. "He believed standardizing the response would reduce liability."

"Standardization is useful when the things being standardized are the same."

"He thought your exception was an attempt to preserve a proprietary experiment."

"The field drive is physically disconnected. We were preserving a load path."

"He understands that now."

"Does he?"

Sloane considered the question. "He understands that two people were injured and his directive has been suspended. Whether he understands the engineering will take longer."

Aaron rubbed his eyes.

"Tell him he's welcome to come learn. He can start by reading the revision block."

Sloane almost smiled.

***

At 16:40, Jian brought a different kind of bad news.

The unauthorized valve movement had not damaged the experimental coil circuit. It had, however, contaminated the facility's remaining thermal reserve. The conditioning system had worked harder than planned to correct the differential, and the emergency buffer tank now held less useful temperature margin than its nominal capacity suggested.

The normal service loop was still running. Its controls remained in a qualified safe state. The reserve was not yet being consumed at the critical rate.

But a scheduled utility inspection, now unavoidable after the unauthorized access, would require a controlled transfer to the independent buffer circuit. The transfer could not occur until a staged plan was approved. Once it began, the remaining buffer would have a finite duration under the conservative worst-case heat load.

"How finite?" Aaron asked.

Jian put the calculation on the display. He had used the measured tank temperature distribution rather than the nameplate volume, subtracted unusable mixing layers, bounded the heat leak, and included the mechanical ramp limit.

"Thirteen minutes, give or take our uncertainty, before we must have the next safe state established. Not thirteen minutes until an explosion. Thirteen minutes until the buffer can no longer guarantee the required thermal band."

Aaron studied the number.

The calculation was not a countdown yet. No transfer had been ordered. There was time to plan, to inspect, and to move people away from every area that did not need to remain occupied.

"Then nobody starts that transfer until we have a phased evacuation, a second verified heat path, and an abort plan," he said.

"Agreed," Hale said.

ASTRA added, "And until you agree that your presence inside the restricted hall is not a required component."

Aaron looked at her. "You already have my answer."

"I would like to hear it."

"I'm not a spare part."

She held his gaze. "No. You are not."

He reached toward the light of her hand. She aligned her fingers with his because she wanted to, not because a protocol asked her to.

Outside, the last Directorate truck rolled away from the gate. Inside, PG-1 remained dark, injured, and stubbornly physical. Bellwether's evacuated streets remained empty. No one had proved a Curator attack or a new law of nature. They had proved that a rescue plan could become a hazard when its authors mistook a diagram for the machine.

Thirteen minutes was not enough time to improvise a miracle.

It was enough time to execute a plan they had not yet written.

Aaron turned back to the engineering board.

"All right," he said. "Let's write the one that works."
